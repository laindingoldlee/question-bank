\textbf{No.} (The difference-of-squares idea gives $a^2-\left(\sqrt{b^2+c^2}\right)^2$, but $\sqrt{b^2+c^2}$ is not a polynomial.)

Suppose $a^2-b^2-c^2=(pa+qb+rc)(sa+tb+uc)$ with real coefficients. (If the factors had constant terms $k_1$ and $k_2$, the product would have constant term $k_1k_2=0$, say $k_2=0$; then its degree-$1$ part would be $k_1$ times the second linear form, which must vanish, so $k_1=0$ too. So the factors may be taken without constant terms.) Comparing coefficients:
\[
ps=1,\quad qt=-1,\quad ru=-1,\quad pt+qs=0,\quad pu+rs=0,\quad qu+rt=0 .
\]
From $ps=1$, $s=\dfrac{1}{p}$. Then $pt+\dfrac {q}{p}=0$ gives $t=-\dfrac{q}{p^2}$, and $qt=-1$ gives $q^2=p^2$. In the same way $u=-\dfrac{r}{p^2}$ and $r^2=p^2$. Now
\[
qu+rt=-\dfrac{qr}{p^2}-\dfrac{rq}{p^2}=-\dfrac{2qr}{p^2},
\]
which is not $0$ because $q^2=r^2=p^2\ne0$. This contradiction shows that $a^2-b^2-c^2$ \textbf{cannot} be factored into linear factors (it is irreducible).
