Multiply the two equations:
\[
\left(x+\dfrac{1}{y}\right)\left(y+\dfrac{1}{x}\right)=xy+1+1+\dfrac{1}{xy}=\dfrac{1}{5}\cdot20=4 .
\]
With $p=xy$ this is $p+2+\dfrac{1}{p}=4$, i.e.\ $p^2-2p+1=0$, so $(p-1)^2=0$ and $p=1$.

\textbf{Such $x,y$ exist.} With $xy=1$ we have $\dfrac{1}{y}=x$, so the first equation reads $2x=\dfrac{1}{5}$: $x=\dfrac{1}{10}$, $y=10$. Indeed $\dfrac{1}{10}+\dfrac{1}{10}=\dfrac{1}{5}$ and $10+10=20$. Hence
\[
xy=\boxed{1}.
\]
\textbf{Why isolating $x$ and $y$ is awkward.} From $x=\dfrac{1}{5}-\dfrac{1}{y}$ and $y=20-\dfrac{1}{x}$ the product is
\[
xy=\left(\dfrac{1}{5}-\dfrac{1}{y}\right)\left(20-\dfrac{1}{x}\right)=4-\dfrac{1}{5x}-\dfrac{20}{y}+\dfrac{1}{xy},
\]
which still contains $x$ and $y$ separately (the post's version has $\dfrac{20}{x}$ in place of $\dfrac{20}{y}$, a slip). That route can be finished (it amounts to solving for $x$ and $y$), but multiplying the original equations makes every term depend on $xy$ alone, which is why the book's method is so quick.
