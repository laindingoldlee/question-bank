\textbf{The triangle is acute.}

Since $c^3=a^3+b^3>a^3$ and $c^3>b^3$, the side $c$ is the longest, so the largest angle is the angle $C$ opposite $c$. It suffices to show that $C$ is acute, that is (by the law of cosines, $c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C$) that $c^2<a^2+b^2$.

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(4cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
real a=1.0,b=1.15,c=(a^3+b^3)^(1/3);
pair C=(0,0),B=(a,0);
real cosC=(a^2+b^2-c^2)/(2*a*b);
pair A=b*(cosC,sqrt(1-cosC^2));
draw(A--B--C--cycle,linewidth(0.9));
draw(arc(C,0.15,0,degrees(A)),red);
label("$A$",A,N); label("$B$",B,SE); label("$C$",C,SW);
label("$a$",(B+C)/2,S); label("$b$",(A+C)/2,NW); label("$c$",(A+B)/2,NE);
label("\mbox{}",(min(currentpicture,user=true)+max(currentpicture,user=true))/2,black); // keeps the text after the figure black when LaTeX typesets the labels inline
\end{asy}

Because $a<c$ and $b<c$,
\[
c^3=a^3+b^3=a\cdot a^2+b\cdot b^2<c\,a^2+c\,b^2=c\,(a^2+b^2),
\]
and dividing by $c>0$ gives $c^2<a^2+b^2$. Hence $\cos C>0$, the largest angle is acute, and so the triangle is acute. (Such triangles exist: $c<a+b$ holds automatically since $(a+b)^3>a^3+b^3$.)
