Taking $d=1$ shows $n+1$ is a square. The values $n=1,4$ fail immediately. For every composite $n>4$, we have $n\mid(n-1)!$: if $n=uv$ with $1<u<v<n$, both factors occur in the factorial; if $n=u^2$ with $u\ge3$, the distinct factors $u,2u<n$ have product $2n$.
Now take $d=n$. The square $n+n!=n(1+(n-1)!)$ is a product of coprime positive integers, since the second factor is $1\pmod n$. Hence $n$ itself must be a square. But then $n$ and $n+1$ would be consecutive positive squares, impossible.
Thus $n$ is prime. Writing $n+1=k^2$ gives $n=(k-1)(k+1)$, so primality forces $k=2$ and $n=3$. Its divisors are $1,3$, and $3+1!=4$, $3+3!=9$. Therefore $\boxed{n=3}$.
