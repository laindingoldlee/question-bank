If $p$ is the smallest prime factor of $n$, then $D(n)=\dfrac {n}{p}$. We look for pairs with $n=5a$, where $a$ is odd and not divisible by $3$; then the smallest prime factor of $n$ is $5$, so $D(n)=a$, and $n+1=5a+1$ is even, so $D(n+1)=\dfrac{5a+1}{2}$. The pair is square when
\[
a+\dfrac{5a+1}{2}=\dfrac{7a+1}{2}=k^2\qquad\text{for an integer }k,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad a=\dfrac{2k^2-1}{7}.
\]

\textbf{Choice of $k$.} Take $k\equiv2\pmod7$, $k\ge9$, that is $k=7m+2$ with $m\ge1$. Then $2k^2-1=98m^2+56m+7=7(14m^2+8m+1)$, so
\[
a=14m^2+8m+1 .
\]
\begin{itemize}
\item $a$ is odd, so $n+1=5a+1$ is even, and $n+1\ge4$ is composite.
\item $a$ is not divisible by $3$: modulo $3$, $a\equiv2m^2+2m+1$, which is $1$, $2$, $1$ for $m\equiv0,1,2$, never $0$.
\item $a>1$, so $n=5a$ is composite, and its smallest prime factor is $5$ (it is odd and not divisible by $3$).
\end{itemize}
So $D(n)=a$, $D(n+1)=\dfrac{5a+1}{2}$, and $D(n)+D(n+1)=\dfrac{7a+1}{2}=k^2$. Different $m$ give different $n$, so there are infinitely many square pairs. $\blacksquare$

For $m=1$: $k=9$, $a=23$, $n=115$: $D(115)=23$, $D(116)=58$, and $23+58=81=9^2$. (The example $(35,36)$ is the case $k=5$, $a=7$.)
