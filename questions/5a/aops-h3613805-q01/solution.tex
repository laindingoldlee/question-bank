For a prime exponent $e>0$, the number of ordered allocations among three entries having minimum exponent zero is $\binom{e+2}2-\binom{e-1}2=3e$; for $e=0$ it is one. The gcd can only be $1,2,3,5$. Dividing all entries by that gcd gives respectively
\[
(9)(15)(21)=2835,\quad(1)(15)(21)=315,\quad(9)(6)(21)=1134,\quad(9)(15)(12)=1620
\]
ordered triples, totaling $5904$.
A fixed transposition requires two entries equal, say $a=b=2^i3^j5^k$. The third entry then has exponents $(3-2i,5-2j,7-2k)$. The gcd condition allows precisely
\[
(i,j,k)=(0,0,0),(0,0,1),(0,0,3),(0,1,0),(0,2,0),(1,0,0).
\]
There are six such fixed triples per transposition. No triple is fixed by a three-cycle, because all three entries would be equal and the product is not a cube. Burnside\'s lemma now gives
\[
\boxed{\dfrac{5904+3\cdot6}{6}=987}.
\]
If three distinct entries were required, the six repeated-entry triples would be removed, giving $981$.
