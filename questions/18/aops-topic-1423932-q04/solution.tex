We use Girard's theorem: \emph{a spherical triangle with angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ (in radians) on a sphere of radius $R$ has area $\left(\alpha+\beta+\gamma-\pi\right)R^2$.}

\textbf{Proof of the theorem.} Two great circles through a point $A$ meeting at angle $\alpha$ cut the sphere into four \emph{lunes}; a lune of angle $\alpha$ is the fraction $\dfrac{\alpha}{2\pi}$ of the sphere, so its area is $\dfrac{\alpha}{2\pi}\cdot4\pi R^2=2\alpha R^2$. Let $T$ be the triangle $ABC$ and $T'$ its antipodal triangle (all points opposite to points of $T$). The three great circles through the sides of $T$ give, at $A$, a lune containing $T$ and the opposite lune containing $T'$, with total area $4\alpha R^2$; similarly $4\beta R^2$ at $B$ and $4\gamma R^2$ at $C$. These six lunes cover the sphere, covering each point of $T$ and of $T'$ exactly three times and every other point exactly once. Hence
\[
4(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)R^2=4\pi R^2+2\cdot[T]+2\cdot[T'] =4\pi R^2+4[T],
\]
so $[T]=(\alpha+\beta+\gamma-\pi)R^2$. $\blacksquare$

\textbf{Answer.} Here $\alpha=\beta=\gamma=72^\circ=\dfrac{2\pi}{5}$, so
\[
[T]=\left(\dfrac{6\pi}{5}-\pi\right)R^2=\dfrac{\pi}{5}R^2=\dfrac{1}{20}\cdot4\pi R^2 .
\]
The triangle covers $\dfrac{1}{20}$ of the sphere, that is, $\boxed{5\%}$.

Check: projecting the $20$ faces of a regular icosahedron onto its circumscribed sphere gives $20$ congruent spherical triangles with angles $\dfrac{360^\circ}{5}=72^\circ$ (five meet at each vertex), each covering $\dfrac{1}{20}$ of the sphere. The figure shows one of them with the three great circles through its sides (dotted on the back of the sphere).
