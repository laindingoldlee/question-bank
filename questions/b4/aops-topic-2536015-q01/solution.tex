Let $s_k$ be the number of chocolates eaten in the first $k$ days, with $s_0=0$. Since he eats at least one each day,
\[0=s_0<s_1<s_2<\cdots<s_{58}=100.\]
These are $59$ different integers from $0$ to $100$. Split the integers $0,1,\dots,100$ into $58$ groups:
\[\{0,58\},\{1,59\},\dots,\{42,100\}\ (43\text{ pairs}),\qquad\{43\},\{44\},\dots,\{57\}\ (15\text{ single numbers}).\]
By the pigeonhole principle two of the $59$ numbers $s_k$ lie in the same group. A single-number group cannot hold two different numbers, so they are a pair $\{t,t+58\}$: $s_i=t$ and $s_j=t+58$ for some $i<j$. Then on days $i+1,i+2,\dots,j$ he ate $s_j-s_i=58$ chocolates. $\blacksquare$
