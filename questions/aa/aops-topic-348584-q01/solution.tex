After the turn he walks in the direction $60^\circ$ north of east. Put the start at the origin, with east along the positive $x$-axis. After the first leg he is at $(4,0)$; the second leg adds $6(\cos60^\circ,\sin60^\circ)=(3,3\sqrt3)$, so he ends at $(7,3\sqrt3)$.

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");

draw(O--P--Q,linewidth(1));
draw(O--Q,red+dashed);
draw(P--(8.2,0),gray);draw(Q--(Q.x,0),gray+dashed);
draw(arc(P,0.8,0,60));label("$60^\circ$",P+1.25*dir(30));

real q=0.25;draw((Q.x-q,0)--(Q.x-q,q)--(Q.x,q),gray);
dot(O);dot(P);dot(Q);
label("start",O,W);label("$4$",(O+P)/2,S);label("$6$",(P+Q)/2,E);label("$3$",((P.x+Q.x)/2,0),S,gray);
label("$3\sqrt{3}$",(Q.x,Q.y/2),E,gray);

His distance from the start is
\[
\sqrt{7^2+\left(3\sqrt3\right)^2}=\sqrt{49+27}=\boxed{2\sqrt{19}}\text{ miles}.
\]
Equivalently, in the triangle formed by the two legs the angle at the turning point is $180^\circ-60^\circ=120^\circ$, and the law of cosines gives $4^2+6^2-2\cdot4\cdot6\cos120^\circ=16+36+24=76$.

The answer $2\sqrt7$ comes from using $60^\circ$ as the angle \emph{between} the two legs ($16+36-24=28$). But turning through $60^\circ$ changes the direction by $60^\circ$, so the angle inside the triangle at the turning point is the supplement, $120^\circ$.
