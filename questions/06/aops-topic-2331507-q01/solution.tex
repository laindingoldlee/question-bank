Describe a position by the amount $r=n-(\text{current total})$ still missing, for the player about to move. A move decreases $r$ by $1$, $3$ or $4$ (not below $0$), and the player who makes $r=0$ wins. Call $r$ \emph{losing} if the player to move loses against best play.

\textbf{Claim.} $r$ is losing exactly when $r\equiv0$ or $2\pmod 7$.

We prove the two facts that establish this (by induction on $r$):
\begin{itemize}
\item From $r\equiv0$ or $2\pmod7$, every move leads to $r\not\equiv0,2$. From $r\equiv0$ the moves give $r-1,r-3,r-4\equiv6,4,3$; from $r\equiv2$ they give $1,6,5\pmod7$. (For $r=0$ the game is already over: the previous player has announced $n$.)
\item From $r\not\equiv0,2\pmod7$, some move leads to $r\equiv0$ or $2$: if $r\equiv1,3,4$, subtract $1,3,4$ respectively to reach $r\equiv0$; if $r\equiv5$ or $6$, subtract $3$ or $4$ respectively to reach $r\equiv2$. These moves are legal, because $r$ is at least the amount subtracted.
\end{itemize}
So a player facing $r\equiv0,2$ can only hand the opponent a winning position, while a player facing any other $r$ can hand the opponent a losing one; since $r$ decreases, the player who always moves to $r\equiv0,2\pmod7$ eventually reaches $r=0$ and wins.

At the start $A$ faces $r=n$. Hence $B$ has a winning strategy exactly when
\[\boxed{n\equiv0\text{ or }2\pmod 7},\]
that is, $n=2,7,9,14,16,21,\dots$; for all other $n$ the first player wins. (For $n=12\equiv5$, $A$ wins by first announcing $3$, leaving $r=9$.)
