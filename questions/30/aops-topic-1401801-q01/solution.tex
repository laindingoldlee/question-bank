\textbf{(a)} Two sides of a triangle share a vertex. If they had the same slope, they would lie on the same line through that vertex, and the three vertices would be collinear, which is impossible for a triangle.

\textbf{(b)} Let $A=(0,0)$ and $B=(1,m_1)$, so $AB$ has slope $m_1$. Let $\ell$ be the line through $B$ with slope $m_2$, $y=m_1+m_2(x-1)$, and let $k$ be the line through $A$ with slope $m_3$, $y=m_3x$. Since $m_2\ne m_3$ they meet, at the point $C$ with
\[
m_3x=m_1+m_2(x-1)\iff x=\dfrac{m_1-m_2}{m_3-m_2}.
\]
This $x$ is not $0$ (as $m_1\ne m_2$), so $C\ne A$; and $C$ is not on line $AB$, because the only common point of $k$ and line $AB$ is $A$ (the slopes $m_3\ne m_1$ differ). So $A$, $B$, $C$ form a triangle with $AB$, $BC$, $CA$ of slopes $m_1$, $m_2$, $m_3$. For example, $m_1=\dfrac{1}{2}$, $m_2=-2$, $m_3=3$ give (after scaling by $2$ and a translation) the triangle below.

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(4.5cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
import graph;
pair A=(1.2,0.8),B=(3.2,1.8),C=(2.2,3.8);
draw((-0.4,0)--(4,0),Arrow(TeXHead)); draw((0,-0.4)--(0,4.4),Arrow(TeXHead));
label("$x$",(4,0),E); label("$y$",(0,4.4),N);
draw(A--B--C--cycle,blue+linewidth(1));
dot(A);dot(B);dot(C);
label("$A$",A,SW); label("$B$",B,E); label("$C$",C,NE);
label("slope $\dfrac{1}{2}$",(A+B)/2,SE,fontsize(9pt));
label("slope $-2$",(B+C)/2,E,fontsize(9pt));
label("slope $3$",(A+C)/2,W,fontsize(9pt));
label("\mbox{}",(min(currentpicture,user=true)+max(currentpicture,user=true))/2,black); // keeps the text after the figure black when LaTeX typesets the labels inline
\end{asy}

So there is \textbf{no} special relation: the only restriction on the slopes (of non-vertical sides) is that they are distinct, and if one side is vertical the other two slopes can likewise be any two distinct numbers.
