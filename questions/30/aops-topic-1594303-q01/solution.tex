There are $\dbinom{20}{3}=1140$ triangles. Count the complement: triangles all of whose sides lie on the surface. Since the dodecahedron is convex, a segment joining two vertices lies on the surface exactly when the two vertices lie on a common face; call such a pair \emph{cofacial}. A cofacial pair is either an edge or a diagonal of a face.

\textbf{Cofacial means graph distance at most $2$.} Two vertices of a pentagon are joined by an edge of the pentagon or by a path of two of its edges. Conversely, if $X$--$u$--$Y$ is a path of two edges, then $Xu$ and $uY$ are two of the three edges at $u$, and any two edges at a vertex of the dodecahedron lie in a common face (three faces meet at each vertex, each containing two of the three edges); so $X$ and $Y$ are cofacial. So the cofacial pairs are exactly the pairs at distance $1$ or $2$ in the graph of the dodecahedron (the edges and vertices).

\textbf{Facts about the graph.} The graph has no cycles of length $3$, $4$ or $6$, and its $5$-cycles are exactly the boundaries of the $12$ faces. (These facts are standard, and are confirmed by a computer check of the graph.)

\textbf{Classifying the triangles.} Let $X$, $Y$, $Z$ be pairwise cofacial. Joining them by shortest paths gives a closed walk $X\to Y\to Z\to X$ of length $3$, $4$, $5$ or $6$.
\begin{itemize}
\item If at least two of the three distances are $1$, say $XY$ and $YZ$ are edges, then $X$--$Y$--$Z$ is a path of two edges, which lies in a face; so $X,Y,Z$ lie on one face. (All three distances $1$ would be a $3$-cycle, which does not exist.)
\item If exactly one distance is $1$, the closed walk has length $5$. A closed walk of odd length contains a cycle of odd length at most $5$, and the only one available is a $5$-cycle, which must be the whole walk. So the walk is the boundary of a face, and $X,Y,Z$ lie on one face.
\item If all three distances are $2$, the closed walk $X-a-Y-b-Z-c-X$ has length $6$. There are no $6$-cycles and no shorter cycles that could fit in an even walk of length $6$ (a $5$-cycle would leave one more edge, which cannot close up), so the walk only goes back and forth along a tree. A tree containing $X,Y,Z$ with all three pairwise distances $2$ is a star: $a=b=c$ is a common neighbour. So $X,Y,Z$ are the three neighbours of one vertex.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Counting.} Triangles with all three vertices on one face: $12\cdot\dbinom53=120$ (no triangle lies in two faces, since two faces share at most two vertices). Triangles formed by the three neighbours of a vertex: $20$, one for each vertex; they are not on one face, and different vertices give different triangles (the three neighbours of $v$ have only $v$ as a common neighbour). So $120+20=140$ triangles lie entirely on the surface, and
\[
1140-140=\boxed{1000}
\]
triangles have at least one side through the interior.
