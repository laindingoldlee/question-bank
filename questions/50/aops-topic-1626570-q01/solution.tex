\textbf{The chance that a birthday is a Friday the 13th.} A non-leap year has twelve $13$ths. If the year is equally likely to begin on each day of the week, each of these twelve days is a Friday with probability $\dfrac{1}{7}$. So a uniformly random birthday is a Friday the $13$th with probability
\[
p=\dfrac{12}{365}\cdot\dfrac{1}{7}=\dfrac{12}{2555}.
\]

\textbf{Conditioning.} Call a child \emph{special} if it is a girl born on a Friday the $13$th; each child is special with probability $\dfrac{p}{2}$, independently.
\[
P(\text{at least one special})=1-\left(1-\dfrac{p}{2}\right)^2=p-\dfrac{p^2}{4}.
\]
Both children are girls and at least one is special exactly when both are girls ($\dfrac{1}{4}$) and at least one of the two girls has the birthday property:
\[
P(\text{two girls and at least one special})=\dfrac{1}{4}\left(1-(1-p)^2\right)=\dfrac{2p-p^2}{4}.
\]
Hence
\[
P(\text{two girls}\mid\text{at least one special})=\dfrac{\dfrac{2p-p^2}{4}}{p-\dfrac{p^2}{4}}=\dfrac{2-p}{4-p}.
\]
With $p=\dfrac{12}{2555}$,
\[
\dfrac{2-p}{4-p}=\dfrac{5110-12}{10220-12}=\dfrac{5098}{10208}=\boxed{\dfrac{2549}{5104}}\approx0.4994 .
\]

\textbf{Remark.} The answer is neither $\dfrac{1}{3}$ (the answer when we only know that at least one child is a girl) nor $\dfrac{1}{2}$, but very close to $\dfrac{1}{2}$: the rare extra detail makes it unlikely that both children are girls and \emph{both} have the detail, so the two children become almost independent of each other.
