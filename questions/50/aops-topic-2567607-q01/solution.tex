Write $n=10m+d$ with $d\in\{0,\dots,9\}$, so $\left\lfloor\dfrac n{10}\right\rfloor=m$. If $m=0$, then $n=d\ge1$ but $m=0$, which fails. For $m\ge1$, the digit sum of $n$ is $S(m)+d$, where $S(m)$ is the digit sum of $m$. The condition is
\[S(m)+d=m\iff d=m-S(m).\]
Now $m-S(m)$ is a nonnegative multiple of $9$ (as $m\equiv S(m)\pmod9$), and $d\le9$, so $d=0$ or $d=9$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $d=0$: $m=S(m)$ holds exactly for one-digit $m$ (a number with at least two digits exceeds its digit sum by at least $9$). This gives $n=10,20,\dots,90$: $9$ numbers.
\item $d=9$: $m-S(m)=9$. Writing $m$ in digits, $m-S(m)=9t_1+99t_2+999t_3+\cdots$ with $t_1$ the tens digit, $t_2$ the hundreds digit, and so on, so $t_1=1$ and the higher digits are $0$: $m=10,\dots,19$. This gives $n=109,119,\dots,199$: $10$ numbers.
\end{itemize}
The total is $9+10=\boxed{19}$.
