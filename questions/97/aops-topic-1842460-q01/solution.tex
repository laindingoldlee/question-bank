We prove that $\gcd\left(2^a-1,2^b-1\right)=\boxed{2^{\gcd(a,b)}-1}$.

\textbf{Key step.} If $a>b$, then
\[
2^a-1=2^{a-b}\left(2^b-1\right)+\left(2^{a-b}-1\right),
\]
so every common divisor of $2^a-1$ and $2^b-1$ divides $2^{a-b}-1$, and every common divisor of $2^{a-b}-1$ and $2^b-1$ divides $2^a-1$. Hence
\[
\gcd\left(2^a-1,\,2^b-1\right)=\gcd\left(2^{a-b}-1,\,2^b-1\right).
\]

\textbf{Induction.} We prove the claim by strong induction on $a+b$. If $a=b$, both numbers equal $2^a-1$ and $\gcd(a,b)=a$, so the claim holds. If $a\ne b$, say $a>b$, the key step and the induction hypothesis (for the pair $a-b$, $b$, with smaller sum) give
\[
\gcd\left(2^a-1,2^b-1\right)=\gcd\left(2^{a-b}-1,2^b-1\right)=2^{\gcd(a-b,b)}-1=2^{\gcd(a,b)}-1,
\]
because $\gcd(a-b,b)=\gcd(a,b)$. $\blacksquare$

In other words, the exponents go through exactly the steps of the (subtractive) Euclidean algorithm for $a$ and $b$. For example, $\gcd\left(2^{12}-1,2^{18}-1\right)=2^6-1=63$.
