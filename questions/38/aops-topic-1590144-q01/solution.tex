\textbf{The third angle.} The angles add up to $180^\circ$, so $\angle C=180^\circ-82^\circ-64^\circ=\boxed{34^\circ}$.

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(4.5cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
real c=60, a=60*Sin(82)/Sin(34), bb=60*Sin(64)/Sin(34);
pair A=(0,0),B=(c,0),C=bb*dir(82);
draw(A--B--C--cycle,linewidth(0.9));
label("$A$",A,SW);label("$B$",B,SE);label("$C$",C,N);
label("$c=60$",(A+B)/2,S);
label("$b$",(A+C)/2,W);label("$a$",(B+C)/2,E);
draw(arc(A,9,0,82),red);label("$82^\circ$",A+11*dir(41),dir(41),red);
draw(arc(B,9,116,180),red);label("$64^\circ$",B+11*dir(148),dir(148),red);
draw(arc(C,28,262,296),blue);label("$34^\circ$",C+37*dir(279),blue);
label("\mbox{}",(min(currentpicture,user=true)+max(currentpicture,user=true))/2,black); // keeps the text after the figure black when LaTeX typesets the labels inline
\end{asy}

\textbf{The sides.} Two angles and a side are known, so the Law of Sines (not the Law of Cosines) is the efficient tool:
\[
\dfrac{a}{\sin A}=\dfrac{b}{\sin B}=\dfrac{c}{\sin C}=\dfrac{60}{\sin34^\circ}\approx\dfrac{60}{0.55919}\approx107.30 .
\]
Hence
\[
a=107.30\cdot\sin82^\circ\approx107.30\cdot0.99027\approx\boxed{106.3},\qquad
b=107.30\cdot\sin64^\circ\approx107.30\cdot0.89879\approx\boxed{96.4}.
\]
(As a check, the longest side $a$ is opposite the largest angle $A$, and $c$ is opposite the smallest angle $C$.) The Law of Cosines is the right tool only when the data are two sides and the included angle, or three sides.
