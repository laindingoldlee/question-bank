Suppose $P=AB$ with $A,B$ non-constant polynomials. Regard all polynomials as polynomials in $x$ whose coefficients are polynomials in $y$.

\textbf{One factor is linear in $x$.} The degrees in $x$ add up: $\deg_xA+\deg_xB=3$. If, say, $\deg_xA=0$, then $A=A(y)$ divides every coefficient of $P$ as a polynomial in $x$, in particular the leading coefficient $1$ of $x^3$; then $A$ is constant, a contradiction. So the degrees in $x$ are $1$ and $2$. The leading coefficients (in $x$) of $A$ and $B$ multiply to $1$, so both are nonzero constants, and after rescaling we may write $A=x-r(y)$ for a polynomial $r$.

\textbf{Then $r(y)$ is a root.} Substituting $x=r(y)$ makes $A$, and hence $P$, vanish identically:
\[
r^3+r^2+y^3+y^2=0\qquad\text{for all }y .
\]

\textbf{No such $r$ exists.}
\begin{itemize}
\item If $r$ is constant, the term $y^3$ cannot cancel.
\item If $\deg r=d\ge2$, the term of degree $3d\ge6$ in $r^3$ cannot cancel.
\item If $r=ay+b$ with $a\ne0$, compare coefficients:
\[
y^3:\ a^3+1=0,\qquad y^2:\ 3a^2b+a^2+1=0,\qquad y^0:\ b^3+b^2=0 .
\]
The last equation gives $b=0$ or $b=-1$. If $b=0$, then $a^2=-1$, so $a^3=-a$ and $a^3=-1$ gives $a=1$, contradicting $a^2=-1$. If $b=-1$, then $-2a^2+1=0$, so $a^2=\dfrac{1}{2}$, and $a^3=-1$ gives $a=-\dfrac{1}{a^2}=-2$, contradicting $a^2=\dfrac{1}{2}$.
\end{itemize}
Every case is impossible, so $P$ has no factorisation into non-constant polynomials. $\blacksquare$
