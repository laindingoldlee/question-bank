Let $s=a+b+c$. Two identities do the job.

\textbf{Identity 1:} $(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)=s(ab+bc+ca)-abc$. Indeed $a+b=s-c$, $b+c=s-a$, $c+a=s-b$, and
\[
(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)=s^3-(a+b+c)s^2+(ab+bc+ca)s-abc=(ab+bc+ca)s-abc .
\]

\textbf{Identity 2:} $(a+b+c)^3-a^3-b^3-c^3=3(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)$. View the left side as a polynomial in $a$. At $a=-b$ it equals $c^3-(-b)^3-b^3-c^3=0$, so $a+b$ is a factor; by symmetry $b+c$ and $c+a$ are factors too. The left side has degree $3$ (the $a^3$ terms cancel, but it is homogeneous of degree $3$), so it equals $k(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)$ for a constant $k$. Comparing the coefficient of $a^2b$: on the left it is $3$ (from $(a+b+c)^3$), on the right it is $k$. So $k=3$.

\textbf{Conclusion.} The hypothesis says $s(ab+bc+ca)-abc=0$, so by Identity 1, $(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)=0$, and by Identity 2,
\[
(a+b+c)^3-\left(a^3+b^3+c^3\right)=3\cdot0=0 .\qquad\blacksquare
\]
