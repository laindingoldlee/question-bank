Yes. Cut every face into two congruent right isosceles triangles along the diagonal joining its $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ corners in the two varying coordinates. Denote a triangle below that diagonal by L and one above it by U. Pair the twelve triangles as follows:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
\text{piece}&\text{first triangle}&\text{second triangle}\\\hline
A&(x=0,\mathrm L)&(y=1,\mathrm U)\\
B&(x=0,\mathrm U)&(z=1,\mathrm U)\\
C&(x=1,\mathrm L)&(z=0,\mathrm L)\\
D&(x=1,\mathrm U)&(y=0,\mathrm L)\\
E&(y=0,\mathrm U)&(z=1,\mathrm L)\\
F&(y=1,\mathrm L)&(z=0,\mathrm U)
\end{array}
\]
For each face, the first remaining coordinate increases to the right and the second upward. In each pair the triangles share a cube edge, and their right angles occur at opposite ends of that edge. Unfolding the pair across the edge produces a parallelogram with sides $1,\sqrt2$ and angles $45^\circ,135^\circ$. Its area is $1\cdot\sqrt2\sin45^\circ=1$.

All six unfolded pieces are congruent and nonrectangular. Folding each along its shared diagonal restores its two assigned surface triangles, which partition the cube surface exactly.
