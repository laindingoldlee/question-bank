\textbf{(a)} The statement is \textbf{false} for every $n\ge2$. Take $a_k=k$ for $k=1,\dots,n+2$. Then every factor $a_k-a_{k+1}$ equals $-1$, so $A=(-1)^{n+1}=\pm1$, which is not divisible by $n\ge2$. For instance, with $n=2$ and $(a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4)=(1,2,3,4)$ we get $A=-1$, which is odd. (For $n=1$ the statement is trivially true.)

\textbf{(b)} Consider the remainders of $a_1,\dots,a_{n+2}$ on division by $n$. There are only $n$ possible remainders and $n+2>n$ numbers, so by the pigeonhole principle two of the numbers, say $a_i$ and $a_j$ with $i<j$, have the same remainder. Then $n\mid a_i-a_j$, and since $a_i-a_j$ is one of the factors of the product of all differences, $n$ divides that product. $\blacksquare$
