\textbf{One zero.} $f(1)=-3+1+2=0$, so by the factor theorem $x-1$ is a factor:
\[f(x)=-(x-1)\left(3x^3+3x^2+3x+2\right).\]
(Check by expanding: $(x-1)(3x^3+3x^2+3x+2)=3x^4-x-2$.)

\textbf{The cubic factor.} Let $g(x)=3x^3+3x^2+3x+2$ and put $t=x+\dfrac13$. A short computation gives
\[g(x)=3t^3+2t+\dfrac{11}{9}.\]
The function $3t^3+2t$ is strictly increasing, so $g$ has exactly one real zero $t_0$; since $g(-1)=-1<0<2=g(0)$, the zero lies between $x=-1$ and $x=0$, so it differs from $1$. Dividing,
\[3t^3+2t+\dfrac{11}9=(t-t_0)\left(3t^2+3t_0t+3t_0^2+2\right),\]
and the quadratic factor has discriminant $9t_0^2-12\left(3t_0^2+2\right)=-27t_0^2-24<0$, so its two zeros are non-real.

\textbf{Answer.} $f$ has $\boxed{2}$ real zeros ($x=1$ and one zero between $-1$ and $0$, about $-0.80$). Counted with multiplicity it has $\boxed{4}$ complex zeros, as every polynomial of degree $4$ does; the other two are non-real. (A missing $x^3$ or $x^2$ term does not change the number of complex zeros, but the number of \emph{real} zeros can be less than the degree.)
