\textbf{(a)} For $x>0$, $x^2$ takes every value in $(0,\infty)$, so $f(x)=x^2+1$ takes exactly the values $y>1$: the range of $f$ is $(1,\infty)$. Solving $y=x^2+1$ with $x>0$ gives $x=\sqrt{y-1}$ (the positive root, since $x>0$). So
\[
f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt{x-1},\qquad\text{domain }x>1,\qquad\text{range }(0,\infty).
\]
Check: $f^{-1}(f(x))=\sqrt{x^2}=x$ for $x>0$, and $f(f^{-1}(x))=(x-1)+1=x$ for $x>1$.

\textbf{(b)} The inverse undoes $f$, so its inputs are the outputs of $f$ and its outputs are the inputs of $f$: the domain of $f^{-1}$ is the range of $f$, and the range of $f^{-1}$ is the domain of $f$. So a restriction on the domain of $f$ does carry over to the inverse, but as a restriction on its \emph{range} (here, $f^{-1}(x)>0$), and it also changes which values the inverse accepts (here $x>1$). Without a restriction, $x^2+1$ is not one-to-one ($f(2)=f(-2)=5$), so no inverse exists; restricting to $x>0$ (or to $x<0$, which gives $f^{-1}(x)=-\sqrt{x-1}$) makes it one-to-one.
