\textbf{(a)} Look at the situation from the point of view of one suspect, say the first.
\begin{itemize}
\item If the other stays silent: staying silent gives $1$ year, accusing gives $0$ years. Accusing is better.
\item If the other accuses: staying silent gives $4$ years, accusing gives $3$ years. Accusing is better.
\end{itemize}
In both cases accusing is strictly better, so accusing is the better choice whatever the other does (a \emph{dominant strategy}). By symmetry the same holds for the second suspect.

\textbf{(b)} If both follow (a), both accuse and each is jailed for $3$ years. Had both stayed silent, each would have been jailed for only $1$ year. So reasoning that is correct for each suspect separately leads to an outcome that is worse for both: $\boxed{3\text{ years each instead of }1}$.

This situation is the famous \emph{prisoner's dilemma} of game theory: the pair of choices (accuse, accuse) is the only stable outcome (neither suspect can do better by changing only his own choice), yet both prefer (silent, silent).
