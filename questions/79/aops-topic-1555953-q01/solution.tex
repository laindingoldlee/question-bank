We show that the answer is $\boxed{n=1\text{ and every prime }n}$.

\textbf{$n=1$.} Only $a=0$ is allowed, and $1\mid1$. It works.

\textbf{$n$ even, $n\ge4$.} Then $4\mid n!$, and $a^n$ is a perfect square, so $a^n\equiv0$ or $1\pmod4$ and $a^n+1\equiv1$ or $2\pmod 4$. No $a$ works.

\textbf{$n$ odd and composite.} Since $n$ is odd, $a=n!-1$ works: $a^n+1\equiv(-1)^n+1=0\pmod{n!}$. We find a second solution. Let $m=(n-1)!$ and $a=m-1$. By the binomial theorem, as $n$ is odd,
\[
(m-1)^n+1=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom nk m^k(-1)^{n-k}=nm+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\binom nk m^k(-1)^{n-k}.
\]
Here $nm=n!$, and every other term is divisible by $m^2=\bigl((n-1)!\bigr)^2$. This is a multiple of $n!$ because $n\mid(n-1)!$ for composite $n\ne4$: if $n=rs$ with $1<r<s<n$, both $r$ and $s$ appear in $(n-1)!$; if $n=p^2$ with $p\ge3$, both $p$ and $2p<n$ appear. So $a=(n-1)!-1$ also works, and $0\le(n-1)!-1<n!-1$. There are at least two solutions.

\textbf{$n=p$ prime.} We show $a\equiv-1\pmod{p!}$. Write $p!=\prod q^{e_q}$ over the primes $q\le p$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $q=p$ (and $e_p=1$): by Fermat's little theorem $a^p\equiv a\pmod p$, so $a\equiv-1\pmod p$.
\item $q<p$: from $a^p\equiv-1\pmod{q^{e}}$, the number $-a$ is invertible modulo $q^e$ and $(-a)^p\equiv1$ (as $p$ is odd here). The multiplicative order of $-a$ divides both $p$ and $\varphi(q^e)=q^{e-1}(q-1)$, which is coprime to $p$ because $p\ne q$ and $p>q-1$. So the order is $1$, i.e.\ $a\equiv-1\pmod{q^e}$.
\end{itemize}
By the Chinese remainder theorem $a\equiv-1\pmod{p!}$, so $a=p!-1$ is the only candidate, and it works ($(-1)^p+1=0$ for odd $p$; for $p=2$, $1^2+1=2$). Hence every prime has exactly one solution. $\blacksquare$
