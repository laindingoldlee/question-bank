\textbf{Method 1 (factor theorem).} Regard the left side $L$ as a polynomial in $x$. Putting $x=y$ gives
\[
y(y^2-z^2)+y(z^2-y^2)+z(y^2-y^2)=0 ,
\]
so $x-y$ is a factor. By the same argument (the expression changes sign when two variables are swapped) $x-z$ and $y-z$ are factors. The left side has degree $3$, so $L=k(x-y)(x-z)(y-z)$ for a constant $k$. Compare the coefficients of $x^2y$: on the left it is $-1$ (from $y\cdot(-x^2)$); on the right, $(x-y)(x-z)(y-z)$ contains $x^2y$ with coefficient $1$ (from $x\cdot x\cdot y$). So $k=-1$.

\textbf{Method 2 (expansion).} First,
\[
(x-y)(x-z)=x^2-xz-xy+yz,
\]
and multiplying by $(y-z)$,
\[
(x-y)(x-z)(y-z)=x^2y-x^2z-xy^2+xz^2+y^2z-yz^2 .
\]
The left side expands to
\[
xy^2-xz^2+yz^2-x^2y+x^2z-y^2z ,
\]
which is exactly the negative of the previous line. $\blacksquare$
