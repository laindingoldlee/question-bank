\textbf{(a) Cut and choose.} Alice moves a knife across the cake from one end to the other. The value, to her, of the piece behind the knife changes continuously from $0$ to $1$, so at some position it is exactly $\dfrac{1}{2}$; she cuts there. Bob then takes the piece he prefers. His values of the two pieces add up to $1$, so the piece he prefers is worth at least $\dfrac{1}{2}$ to him. Alice gets the other piece, worth exactly $\dfrac{1}{2}$ to her. Each gets a fair share no matter what the other does.

\textbf{(b) Last diminisher.} We prove by induction on $k$: \emph{for any piece of cake $X$ and any $k$ people, there is a procedure giving each person a part of $X$ worth at least $\dfrac{1}{k}$ of her own value of $X$.} For $k=1$ the person takes $X$.

For $k\ge2$: person $1$ cuts from $X$ a piece $Y$ worth exactly $\dfrac{1}{k}$ of her value of $X$ (possible by continuity). The piece is passed to persons $2,3,\dots,k$ in turn. A person who values the current piece at more than $\dfrac{1}{k}$ of her value of $X$ trims it down to exactly $\dfrac{1}{k}$ (by continuity), returning the trimmings to the rest of $X$; otherwise she passes. Finally the last person who trimmed (or person $1$, if nobody trimmed) takes the piece and leaves.
\begin{itemize}
\item The person who leaves values her piece at exactly $\dfrac{1}{k}$ of $X$.
\item Every other person values the final piece at most $\dfrac{1}{k}$ of $X$: a person who trimmed valued it at exactly $\dfrac{1}{k}$ right after her trim, a person who passed valued it at most $\dfrac{1}{k}$ when she saw it, person $1$ valued the original $Y$ at $\dfrac{1}{k}$, and afterwards the piece only got smaller.
\end{itemize}
So each of the $k-1$ remaining people values the rest $X'$ of $X$ at least $\dfrac{k-1}{k}$ of her value of $X$. By the induction hypothesis they can share $X'$ so that each gets at least $\dfrac{1}{k-1}$ of her value of $X'$, which is at least $\dfrac{1}{k-1}\cdot\dfrac{k-1}{k}=\dfrac{1}{k}$ of her value of $X$. Taking $X$ to be the whole cake and $k=n$, every person gets at least $\dfrac{1}{n}$. $\blacksquare$

\textbf{(c)} For $n=2$, the first cut alone does not divide a round cake; the second cut makes two pieces, and the first cutter chooses first. So the second cutter can rotate her cut until the two pieces are worth $\dfrac{1}{2}$ each to her (continuity again), and then she gets $\dfrac{1}{2}$ whichever piece the other takes. This is cut and choose, and both get fair shares (the first cutter, choosing first, gets at least $\dfrac{1}{2}$).

For $n=3$ the method can fail. Suppose all three people value the cake by area. Person $1$ cuts along some radius, and person $2$ cuts along the radius at $36^\circ$ from it, making pieces of $36^\circ$ and $324^\circ$. Person $3$ makes one more cut, which splits one of these two pieces. Whatever she does, there are three pieces and the smallest is at most $36^\circ$: if she splits the large piece, the $36^\circ$ piece remains, and if she splits the small one, its parts are smaller still. Persons $1$ and $2$ choose before her and may take the two largest pieces, leaving her at most $\dfrac{36}{360}=\dfrac{1}{10}<\dfrac{1}{3}$ of the cake. So the method does not guarantee a fair share to the last cutter.
