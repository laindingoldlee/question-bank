\textbf{(a)} The square roots of $w$ are $\pm r$ for some $r$, and the principal one is chosen to have positive real part, or real part $0$ and nonnegative imaginary part. The number $-1$ has real part $-1<0$, so it is never the chosen root: if $-1$ is a square root of $w$, then $w=(-1)^2=1$, whose square roots are $1$ and $-1$, and the principal one is $1$.

\textbf{(b)} If $-1$ is a square root of $j$, then by the usual rules $j=(-1)^2=(-1)(-1)=1$. So $j$ is not new at all: it is the number $1$, and the principal square root of $1$ is $1$, not $-1$. Declaring $\sqrt j=-1$ therefore contradicts either the rules of arithmetic or the definition of $\sqrt{\ }$; no new number can do it. (In this sense $-1$ already has the ``number'' it would be the root of, namely $1$, but $-1$ is the non-principal root.)

With $i$ the situation is different: no real number $x$ satisfies $x^2=-1$, so requiring $i^2=-1$ does not clash with any existing fact. One can construct the complex numbers as pairs of real numbers $(a,b)=a+bi$ with a suitable multiplication, and check that all the usual rules hold; so $i$ is consistent. Calling $i$ ``$\sqrt{-1}$'' is then a convention choosing $i$ rather than $-i$ as the principal root. $\blacksquare$
