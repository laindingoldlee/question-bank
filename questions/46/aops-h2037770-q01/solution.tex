Put $A=(0,0)$ and the internal bisector on the horizontal axis. With $b=AC,c=AB$, write
\[
B=(c\cos\theta,c\sin\theta),\quad C=(b\cos\theta,-b\sin\theta),
\quad2\theta=\angle A.
\]
Let $a=BC$. Standard angle-bisector coordinates, obtained by weighting the vertices by their opposite side lengths, give
\[
I=(u,0),\quad I_a=(v,0),\qquad
u=\dfrac{2bc\cos\theta}{a+b+c},\quad
v=\dfrac{2bc\cos\theta}{b+c-a}.
\]
The cosine law implies $(b+c)^2-a^2=4bc\cos^2\theta$, so $uv=bc$.

The external bisector is the vertical axis, which contains $K,E$. Intersecting it with $BC$ gives
\[
K=\left(0,\dfrac{2bc\sin\theta}{b-c}\right).
\]
The circumcircle through the origin has equation $x^2+y^2-2O_xx-2O_yy=0$. Its other vertical-axis intersection is
\[
E=\left(0,\dfrac{c-b}{2\sin\theta}\right);
\]
this is the specified arc midpoint, since the external bisector intercepts the equal arcs there. Consequently $K_yE_y=-bc=-uv$.

Thus $EK\perp II_a$. Also
\[
(K-I)\cdot(E-I_a)=(-u,K_y)\cdot(-v,E_y)=uv+K_yE_y=0,
\]
so $IK\perp EI_a$. Two altitudes of triangle $II_aE$ pass through $K$, proving the claim.
