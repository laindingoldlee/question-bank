Let $h$ be the distance between $AB$ and the line $\ell$ along which $P$ moves; it stays the same.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] By the midline theorem $MN=\dfrac{1}{2}AB$: does not change.
\item[(c)] $[PAB]=\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot AB\cdot h$: does not change.
\item[(d)] $MN\parallel AB$ and $MN$ is at distance $\dfrac{h}{2}$ from $AB$, so $[ABNM]=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(AB+\dfrac{AB}{2}\right)\dfrac{h}{2}=\dfrac{3}{8}AB\cdot h$ (equivalently, $\dfrac{3}{4}$ of the triangle): does not change.
\item[(b)] The perimeter is $AB+PA+PB$, and $PA+PB$ is \emph{not} constant.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Why the perimeter changes.} Put $A=(0,0)$, $B=(c,0)$ and $P=(x,h)$. Then
\[
PA+PB=\sqrt{x^2+h^2}+\sqrt{(x-c)^2+h^2}.
\]
For $x=\dfrac{c}{2}$ this is $2\sqrt{\dfrac{c^2}{4}+h^2}$, while for large $x$ it is larger than $2x-c$ (because $PA>x$ and $PB>x-c$), which grows without bound. So as $P$ slides along $\ell$, the base and the height stay the same (keeping the area and the midline fixed) but the two slanted sides get longer or shorter; the perimeter is smallest when $P$ is directly above the midpoint of $AB$ (by reflecting $A$ in $\ell$ and using the triangle inequality). In the figure, the dashed triangle $ABP'$ has the same area as $ABP$ but a much larger perimeter.

Exactly one quantity changes: $\boxed{\text{(B) }1}$.
