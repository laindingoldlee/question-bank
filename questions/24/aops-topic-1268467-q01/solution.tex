A pathway from the upper-left to the lower-right square must move at least $3$ squares to the right and $2$ squares down, so it uses at least $3+2+1=6$ squares. Three dominoes cover exactly $6$ squares, so the pathway is a shortest path: it moves only right and down.

Conversely, every right/down path of $6$ squares can be covered by three dominoes in exactly one way: the dominoes must cover squares $1$--$2$, $3$--$4$ and $5$--$6$ of the path (in a right/down path, two squares sharing a side are consecutive on the path, and the end square $1$ can only pair with square $2$, and so on). Consecutive dominoes then share a side, so the corner rule is satisfied.

Hence the answer is the number of arrangements of $3$ rights and $2$ downs:
\[\dbinom{5}{2}=\boxed{10}.\]
The same argument works on any $m\times n$ board when $m+n-1$ is even: the answer is $\dbinom{m+n-2}{m-1}$.
