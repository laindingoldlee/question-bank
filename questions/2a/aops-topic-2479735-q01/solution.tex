\textbf{(a)} If $n=2^k$, then $f$ halves it each time: $2^k\to2^{k-1}\to\cdots\to2^0=1$, after $k$ steps.

\textbf{(b)} If $n$ is even and $n>1$, then $f(n)=\dfrac n2<n$. If $n\equiv1\pmod4$ and $n>1$, write $n=4j+1$ with $j\ge1$. Then
\[f(n)=12j+4\ (\text{even}),\qquad f(f(n))=6j+2\ (\text{even}),\qquad f(f(f(n)))=3j+1<4j+1=n.\]

\textbf{(c)} We prove by strong induction on $n$ that every $n$ reaches $1$. For $n=1$ there is nothing to prove. Let $n>1$ and suppose every positive integer smaller than $n$ reaches $1$. By hypothesis, applying $f$ repeatedly to $n$ gives at some point a value $m<n$. By the induction hypothesis, applying $f$ repeatedly to $m$ reaches $1$; continuing the sequence from $n$ past $m$ therefore also reaches $1$. This completes the induction. $\blacksquare$

\emph{Remark.} By (b), the hypothesis of (c) holds for every $n>1$ that is not $\equiv3\pmod4$. Proving it for $n\equiv3\pmod4$ is exactly where the difficulty of the conjecture lies; for example $27$ climbs as high as $9232$ before coming down.
