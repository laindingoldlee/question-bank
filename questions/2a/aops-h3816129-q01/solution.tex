Let $t=\dfrac{1}{3\sqrt{11}}$. The candidate $(a,b,c)=(14t,9t,5t)$ works, since
\[
\sqrt{ab+1}=15t,\quad\sqrt{bc+1}=12t,\quad\sqrt{ca+1}=13t,
\]
using $99t^2=1$. The three equations become $28t=2a$, $27t=3b$, and $25t=5c$.

It remains to prove uniqueness. Define the positive map
\[
F(a,b,c)=\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{ab+1}+\sqrt{ac+1}}{2},\ 
\dfrac{\sqrt{ab+1}+\sqrt{bc+1}}{3},\ 
\dfrac{\sqrt{ac+1}+\sqrt{bc+1}}{5}\right).
\]
It is coordinatewise increasing, and for any positive vector $v$ and $\lambda>1$,
$F(\lambda v)<\lambda F(v)$ in every coordinate, because $\sqrt{\lambda^2z+1}<\lambda\sqrt{z+1}$ for $z>0$.
If two different positive fixed points $u,v$ existed, exchange them if necessary so that $\lambda=\max_i(\dfrac{u_i}{v_i})>1$. Then $u\le\lambda v$, with equality in at least one coordinate, but
$u=F(u)\le F(\lambda v)<\lambda F(v)=\lambda v$, a contradiction.
Thus the candidate is the unique solution and
\[
\boxed{a+b+c=28t=\dfrac{28\sqrt{11}}{33}}.
\]
