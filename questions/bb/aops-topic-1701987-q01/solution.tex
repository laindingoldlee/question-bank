Take the spacing to be $1$, so the possible distances between grid points are $1,\sqrt2,2,\sqrt5,2\sqrt2$. Sort the isosceles triangles by shape.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Legs $1,1$, base $\sqrt2$} (right isosceles in a unit square): each of the $4$ unit squares gives $4$ such triangles (omit one vertex), so $16$.
\item \textbf{Legs $\sqrt2,\sqrt2$, base $2$} (right angle at the apex): the base is a whole row or a whole column of the grid ($3+3=6$ choices), and the apex is a grid point at distance $1$ from the midpoint of the base, perpendicular to it. For the middle row or column there are $2$ such points, for an outer row or column only $1$, so $2\cdot(2+1+1)=8$.
\item \textbf{Legs $2,2$, base $2\sqrt2$} (right isosceles with legs along the sides of the big square): one for each corner of the big square, so $4$.
\item \textbf{Legs $\sqrt5,\sqrt5$, base $2$}: the base is a side of the big square and the apex is the midpoint of the opposite side, so $4$.
\item \textbf{Legs $\sqrt5,\sqrt5$, base $\sqrt2$}: a corner of the big square together with the two points that are a knight's move away from it, such as $(0,0),(1,2),(2,1)$, so $4$.
\end{itemize}
No other combination of two equal sides gives a triangle (for example two sides of length $2\sqrt2$ would need both diagonals of the big square, and three points with two sides of length $1$ along a line are collinear).

The total is $16+8+4+4+4=\boxed{36}$. (The figure shows one example of each shape with its count.)
