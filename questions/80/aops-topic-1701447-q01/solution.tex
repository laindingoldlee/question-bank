Line $BO$ meets $S$ at $B$ and at the antipode $B'$ of $B$. Line $DE$ meets line $BC$ only at $E$, which lies strictly between $B$ and $C$ (the triangle is acute, so $O$ is inside it), so $DE$ does not pass through $B$. Hence the lines meet on $S$ exactly when $DE$ passes through $B'$, i.e.\ when $E$ lies on the chord $DB'$.

pair Ep=extension(A,O,B,C);
pair u=unit(C-B); pair nn=(-u.y,u.x);
pair D=A-2*dot(A,nn)*nn;

\textbf{Where $AO$ meets $BC$.} In an acute triangle $\angle BAO=90^\circ-C$ and $\angle OAC=90^\circ-B$ (triangle $AOB$ is isosceles with apex angle $2C$). Comparing areas of triangles $ABE$ and $AEC$, which have the same height from $A$,
\[
\dfrac{BE}{EC}=\dfrac{AB\sin\angle BAE}{AC\sin\angle EAC}=\dfrac{\sin C\cos C}{\sin B\cos B},
\]
using $AB:AC=\sin C:\sin B$ (law of sines).

\textbf{Where $DB'$ meets $BC$.} $D$ lies on arc $BC$ not containing $A$, and $B'$ lies on arc $CA$ not containing $B$ (since $O$ is inside the triangle), so the chord $DB'$ separates $B$ from $C$ and meets segment $BC$ at a point $F$. In triangle $DBC$, $\angle DBC=\angle DAC=90^\circ-C$ and $\angle DCB=\angle DAB=90^\circ-B$, so by the law of sines $\dfrac{DB}{DC}=\dfrac{\cos B}{\cos C}$. Also $\angle BDF=\angle BDB'=90^\circ$ ($BB'$ is a diameter) and $\angle FDC=\angle B'DC=\angle B'BC=\angle OBC=90^\circ-A$. Comparing areas of triangles $DBF$ and $DFC$,
\[
\dfrac{BF}{FC}=\dfrac{DB\sin90^\circ}{DC\sin(90^\circ-A)}=\dfrac{\cos B}{\cos C\cos A}.
\]

\textbf{Condition.} $E=F$ if and only if $\dfrac{BE}{EC}=\dfrac{BF}{FC}$, that is,
\[
\dfrac{\sin C\cos C}{\sin B\cos B}=\dfrac{\cos B}{\cos C\cos A}
\iff \sin C\cos^2C\cos A=\sin B\cos^2B .
\]
This proves the criterion.

\textbf{Example.} For $A=B=67.5^\circ$, $C=45^\circ$ the condition is $\sin67.5^\circ\cos^267.5^\circ=\sin45^\circ\cos^245^\circ\cos67.5^\circ$; dividing by $\cos67.5^\circ$ it reads $\sin45^\circ\cos^245^\circ=\sin67.5^\circ\cos67.5^\circ$, i.e.\ $\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac{1}{2}=\dfrac{1}{2}\sin135^\circ=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{4}$, which is true. So $\boxed{\text{yes, it is possible}}$; it happens exactly when $\sin B\cos^2B=\sin C\cos^2C\cos A$. (The figure shows this triangle; the red line $DE$ passes through $B'$.)
