\textbf{(a)} Subtract $w$ from both sides of $w+1=w$:
\[
1=1+(w-w)=(w+1)-w=w-w=0,
\]
a contradiction. So no number with the usual rules can satisfy $w+1=w$, while ``$\infty+1=\infty$'' is exactly what we want infinity to do. Hence $\infty$ cannot be treated as a number with all the usual rules; in particular $\infty-\infty$ has no meaning.

\textbf{(b)} Take $a_n=n+c$ and $b_n=n$: both grow without bound and $a_n-b_n=c$, so we get $0$ (with $c=0$) and $5$ (with $c=5$). With $a_n=n^2$ and $b_n=n$, the difference $n^2-n$ grows without bound. So ``$\infty-\infty$'' could be $0$, $5$, or unbounded depending on which quantities are being subtracted: it is \emph{indeterminate}.

\textbf{(c)} $x_n=\dfrac{3}{n}\to0$ and $y_n=n$ give $x_ny_n=3$; $x_n=\dfrac{1}{n^2}$ and $y_n=n$ give $x_ny_n=\dfrac{1}{n}\to0$. So ``$0\cdot\infty$'' is also indeterminate.

\textbf{(d)} Given any $\varepsilon>0$, for all $n>\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}$ we have $0<\dfrac{1}{n}<\varepsilon$; so $\dfrac{1}{n}$ gets and stays as close to $0$ as we like, that is, $\dfrac{1}{n}\to0$. ``$\dfrac{1}{\infty}=0$'' is shorthand for this statement about a limit: if $y_n$ grows without bound, then $\dfrac{1}{y_n}\to0$. It is not an equation between numbers, and unlike $\infty-\infty$ and $0\cdot\infty$ it always gives the same answer.
