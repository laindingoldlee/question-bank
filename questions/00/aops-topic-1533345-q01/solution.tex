\textbf{(a)} By the binomial theorem,
\[
(a+b)^p=a^p+b^p+\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\binom pk a^kb^{p-k}.
\]
For $1\le k\le p-1$ we have $\binom pk=\dfrac{p!}{k!\,(p-k)!}$, an integer. The prime $p$ divides the numerator $p!$ but not $k!\,(p-k)!$, since every factor of that product is less than $p$. As $k!\,(p-k)!\binom pk=p!$ is divisible by $p$ and $p\nmid k!\,(p-k)!$, the prime $p$ divides $\binom pk$. So every term of the sum is divisible by $p$, and $(a+b)^p\equiv a^p+b^p\pmod p$. $\blacksquare$

\textbf{(b)} For $p=4$ and $a=b=1$: $(1+1)^4=16\equiv0\pmod4$, but $1^4+1^4=2\not\equiv0\pmod4$. (Here $\binom42=6$ is not divisible by $4$.)
