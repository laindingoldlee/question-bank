\textbf{What the digital root is.} A number is congruent to the sum of its digits modulo $9$ (because $10^k\equiv1\pmod9$). Each step of the process therefore keeps the residue modulo $9$, and replaces a number with at least two digits by a smaller positive number, so it ends at a single digit from $1$ to $9$. Hence $\operatorname{dr}(n)$ is the unique number in $\{1,2,\dots,9\}$ with $\operatorname{dr}(n)\equiv n\pmod9$.

\textbf{The inequality.} Let $a=\operatorname{dr}(m)$ and $b=\operatorname{dr}(n)$. Then $ab\equiv mn\pmod 9$ and $ab\ge1$. The number $\operatorname{dr}(mn)$ is the \emph{smallest} positive integer congruent to $mn$ modulo $9$ (it lies in $\{1,\dots,9\}$, and the positive members of the residue class are $\operatorname{dr}(mn),\operatorname{dr}(mn)+9,\dots$). Since $ab$ is a positive integer in the same residue class,
\[
\operatorname{dr}(m)\operatorname{dr}(n)=ab\ge\operatorname{dr}(mn). \qquad\blacksquare
\]
(Equality holds exactly when $ab\le9$; for example $\operatorname{dr}(5)\operatorname{dr}(5)=25>7=\operatorname{dr}(25)$.)
