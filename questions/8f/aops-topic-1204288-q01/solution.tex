\textbf{Answer: $10\cdot2^8=2560$.}

\textbf{Key fact.} At every moment the visited cities form a block of consecutive cities on the circle, and the tourist stands at one end of the block.

This is true at the start. Suppose it holds, the tourist is at the end $c$ of the visited block, and the unvisited cities form the complementary arc $u_1,u_2,\dots,u_k$ in circular order, with $u_1$ next to $c$ and $u_k$ next to the other end of the block. Suppose he goes next to $u_j$ with $1<j<k$. The road $cu_j$ splits the circle into two arcs; one contains $u_1,\dots,u_{j-1}$, the other contains $u_{j+1},\dots,u_k$. Both groups still have to be visited, and the cities $c$ and $u_j$ will not be endpoints of any later road except the next one from $u_j$. So at some later moment the route uses a road joining a city on one side of $cu_j$ to a city on the other side, and that road crosses $cu_j$. This is not allowed, so the next city is $u_1$ or $u_k$, and the visited cities again form a block with the tourist at an end.

Conversely, such a move never creates a crossing: all earlier roads join two cities of the visited block, which lies (apart from $c$) entirely on one side of the new road.

\textbf{Counting.} There are $10$ choices for the first city. Each of the next $8$ moves goes to one of the two cities next to the block, and these are different as long as at least two cities are unvisited: $2$ choices each. The last city is then forced. Hence the number of routes is
\[
10\cdot2^8=\boxed{2560}.
\]
(If a route and its reverse are considered the same path, the count is $\dfrac{2560}{2}=1280$.)
