All parts use the same idea: $N$ and the quantity in the rule differ by a multiple of the divisor.

\textbf{(a)} $N=10\left(d_1+10d_2+\cdots\right)+d_0$, and $10$ is divisible by $2$, $5$ and $10$. So $N-d_0$ is a multiple of each of them, and $N$ is divisible by $2$ ($5$, $10$) exactly when $d_0$ is.

\textbf{(b)} $N=100\cdot(\ldots)+\overline{d_1d_0}$ and $4\mid100$, so $N\equiv\overline{d_1d_0}\pmod4$. Likewise $N=1000\cdot(\ldots)+\overline{d_2d_1d_0}$ and $8\mid1000$.

\textbf{(c)} $10\equiv1\pmod9$, so $10^i\equiv1\pmod9$ for every $i$ (each $10^i-1=99\ldots9$ is a multiple of $9$, hence also of $3$). Therefore
\[N-(d_0+d_1+\cdots+d_m)=\sum_i d_i\left(10^i-1\right)\]
is a multiple of $9$, and of $3$, which gives both rules.

\textbf{(d)} If $6\mid N$, then $2\mid N$ and $3\mid N$. Conversely, if $2\mid N$ and $3\mid N$, write $N=2k$; then $3\mid2k$, and as $\gcd(2,3)=1$, $3\mid k$, so $6\mid N$. (Combine with (a) and (c).)

\textbf{(e)} $10\equiv-1\pmod{11}$, so $10^i\equiv(-1)^i\pmod{11}$, and
\[N=\sum_i d_i10^i\equiv\sum_i(-1)^id_i=d_0-d_1+d_2-\cdots\pmod{11}.\]
So $11\mid N$ exactly when $11$ divides the alternating sum. $\blacksquare$

(For $7$ there is no rule of this digit-by-digit kind: the powers $1,10,100,\ldots$ leave the remainders $1,3,2,6,4,5,\ldots$ modulo $7$. Since $10^3\equiv-1\pmod7$, one gets instead a test with alternating sums of blocks of three digits.)
