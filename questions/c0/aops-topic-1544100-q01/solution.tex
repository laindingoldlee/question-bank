The key fact: a common divisor of two numbers also divides their difference, and a common divisor of $a$ and $b$ divides $a+b$. Hence $\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(a,b-a)$.

\textbf{(a) True.} If $d$ divides $x$ and $x+1$, then $d$ divides $(x+1)-x=1$, so $d=1$. Thus $\gcd(x,x+1)=1$.

\textbf{(b) False.} For $x=1$, $\gcd(1,3)=1\ne2$. Correct result: a common divisor of $x$ and $x+2$ divides $2$, so $\gcd(x,x+2)$ is $1$ or $2$; it is $2$ when $x$ is even and $1$ when $x$ is odd.

\textbf{(c) False.} For $x=2$, $y=3$: $\gcd(2,5)=1\ne3$. Correct result: $\gcd(x,x+y)=\gcd(x,y)$, because the common divisors of $x$ and $x+y$ are exactly the common divisors of $x$ and $y$ (subtract or add $x$). In particular $\gcd(x,x+y)=y$ only when $y\mid x$.
