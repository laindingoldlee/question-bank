View a student's classes as a block, and let $k$ be the largest block size. Distinct blocks meet in at most one class.
If $k=6$, the other six blocks must be the six pairs joining the remaining class to each of those six; this is a near-pencil. There are seven choices for its exceptional class.
The case $k=7$ leaves no allowed pair for the other students. If $k=5$, the ten pairs across the large block and its two outside classes cannot be covered by six other blocks: each such block contains at most one inside class, and at most one of them can contain both outsiders, so they cover at most seven cross pairs. If $k=4$, the same six blocks cover at most $6+3=9$ cross pairs, whereas twelve are needed: a block with $t$ outsiders uses $\binom t2\ge t-1$ of the three available outside pairs.
If $k=3$, all seven blocks must be triples to cover all $\binom72=21$ pairs. These are labeled Fano planes. Fix one class. The other six split into three pairs on its three blocks, in $15$ ways. The four remaining triples choose one element from each pair; the pair-coverage condition permits exactly the two parity patterns. Hence there are $30$ such block systems. Finally $k=2$ cannot cover all pairs.
In either valid case the seven distinct blocks can be assigned to students in $7!$ ways. The answer is $\boxed{(7+30)7!=186480}$.
