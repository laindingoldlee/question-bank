\textbf{(a)} Yes. Put $y=f^{-1}(x)$. By the definition of an inverse function, $g\left(g^{-1}(y)\right)=y$ for every $y$, so
\[f\left(g\left(g^{-1}\left(f^{-1}(x)\right)\right)\right)=f(y)=f\left(f^{-1}(x)\right)=\boxed{x}.\]

\textbf{(b)} Let $h=g^{-1}\circ f^{-1}$. Part (a) says $(f\circ g)\bigl(h(x)\bigr)=x$ for all $x$. In the other order,
\[h\bigl((f\circ g)(x)\bigr)=g^{-1}\left(f^{-1}\left(f\left(g(x)\right)\right)\right)=g^{-1}\left(g(x)\right)=x .\]
So $h$ undoes $f\circ g$ on both sides, which means $(f\circ g)^{-1}=g^{-1}\circ f^{-1}$. Note the reversed order: to undo ``first $g$, then $f$'' we first undo $f$ and then undo $g$.

For example, with $f(x)=2x$ and $g(x)=x+1$ we get $(f\circ g)(x)=2x+2$, whose inverse is $\dfrac {x}{2}-1=g^{-1}\left(f^{-1}(x)\right)$, while $f^{-1}\left(g^{-1}(x)\right)=\dfrac{x-1}{2}$ is different.
