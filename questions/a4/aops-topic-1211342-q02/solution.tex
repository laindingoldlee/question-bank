Place the rectangle with corners $(0,0)$, $(4,0)$, $(4,3)$, $(0,3)$ and cut it into five pieces:
\[\begin{array}{ll}
P_1:\ (0,0),(1,0),(1,1.5),(0,2), & P_2:\ (4,0),(3,0),(3,1.5),(4,2),\\
P_3:\ (1,0),(3,0),(3,1),(2,2),(1,1), & \\
P_4:\ (0,2),(1,1.5),(1,1),(2,2),(2,3),(0,3), & P_5:\ (4,2),(3,1.5),(3,1),(2,2),(2,3),(4,3).
\end{array}\]

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(5cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
draw((0,0)--(4,0)--(4,3)--(0,3)--cycle,linewidth(1));
draw((1,0)--(1,1.5)--(0,2)); draw((3,0)--(3,1.5)--(4,2)); draw((1,1)--(2,2)--(3,1)); draw((2,2)--(2,3));
label("$P_1$",(0.45,0.9)); label("$P_2$",(3.55,0.9)); label("$P_3$",(2,0.7)); label("$P_4$",(0.9,2.45)); label("$P_5$",(3.1,2.45));
label("$4$",(2,0),S); label("$3$",(0,1.5),W);
label("\mbox{}",(min(currentpicture,user=true)+max(currentpicture,user=true))/2,black); // keeps the text after the figure black when LaTeX typesets the labels inline
\end{asy}

The diameter of a polygon is the largest distance between two of its vertices, and a direct check shows that in each piece every two vertices are at distance at most $\sqrt5$ (for example in $P_4$: $(0,2)$ to $(2,3)$ and $(0,3)$ to $(2,2)$ and $(1,1)$ to $(0,3)$ are all $\sqrt5$). The pieces cover the rectangle (their areas $1.75+1.75+3+2.75+2.75$ add up to $12$).

Six points in five pieces: by the pigeonhole principle two of them lie in the same piece, and their distance is at most the diameter of that piece, $\sqrt5$.
