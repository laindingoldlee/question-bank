\textbf{(a)} $(a+bi)^3=a^3-3ab^2+\left(3a^2b-b^3\right)i$, so we need
\[a\left(a^2-3b^2\right)=2,\qquad b\left(3a^2-b^2\right)=11.\]
From the first, $a$ divides $2$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $a=1$: $1-3b^2=2$, impossible.
\item $a=-1$: $3b^2-1=2$, so $b=\pm1$; then $b\left(3-b^2\right)=2b=\pm2\ne11$.
\item $a=-2$: $-2\left(4-3b^2\right)=2$ gives $3b^2=5$, impossible.
\item $a=2$: $4-3b^2=1$, so $b=\pm1$, and $b\left(12-b^2\right)=11b=11$ gives $b=1$.
\end{itemize}
So $\boxed{a=2,\ b=1}$: indeed $(2+i)^3=8+12i-6-i=2+11i$.

\textbf{(b)} The argument of $2+11i$ is less than $90^\circ$, so its principal cube root has argument less than $30^\circ$, and $2+i$ (argument about $26.6^\circ$) is that root. Taking complex conjugates, $(2-i)^3=2-11i$, and $2-i$ is the principal cube root of $2-11i$. Therefore
\[\sqrt[3]{2+11i}+\sqrt[3]{2-11i}=(2+i)+(2-i)=\boxed{4}.\]
(This is the real root $x=4$ of $x^3=15x+4$; Cardano's formula writes it as Bombelli's expression.)
