\textbf{Step 1: remove the common factor.} Let $k=\gcd(a,b)$. Every prime appears in $c^2=a^2+b^2$ at least twice as often as in $k$, so it appears in $c$ at least as often as in $k$; hence $k\mid c$. Dividing by $k$ gives a triple $\left(a',b',c'\right)$ with $\gcd(a',b')=1$. It is enough to show that such a triple has the required form with $k=1$; multiplying back by $k$ then gives the general statement.

\textbf{Step 2: parity.} Now $a'$ and $b'$ are not both even. They are not both odd either: then $c'^2=a'^2+b'^2\equiv1+1=2\pmod4$, but squares are $0$ or $1$ modulo $4$. After swapping, $a'$ is odd and $b'$ is even, so $c'$ is odd. Also $\gcd(a',c')=1$, since a prime dividing $a'$ and $c'$ would divide $b'^2$, hence $b'$.

\textbf{Step 3: factor.} Write $b'^2=c'^2-a'^2=(c'-a')(c'+a')$. Both factors are even, so put $c'+a'=2u$ and $c'-a'=2v$ with integers $u>v>0$. Then $\left(\dfrac{b'}{2}\right)^2=uv$. A common divisor of $u$ and $v$ divides $u+v=c'$ and $u-v=a'$, so $\gcd(u,v)=1$. Two coprime positive integers whose product is a perfect square are themselves perfect squares (each prime of the product lies entirely in one of them and appears an even number of times). So $u=m^2$ and $v=n^2$ with $m>n\ge1$, and
\[c'=u+v=m^2+n^2,\qquad a'=u-v=m^2-n^2,\qquad b'=2\sqrt{uv}=2mn .\]
Multiplying by $k$ gives the stated form. $\blacksquare$

\emph{Remarks.} Conversely, every choice of $k$ and $m>n$ gives a triple, because $\left(m^2-n^2\right)^2+(2mn)^2=\left(m^2+n^2\right)^2$. The factor $k$ is needed: for example $(9,12,15)=3\cdot(3,4,5)$ with $m=2$, $n=1$, but with $k=1$ the even leg $12=2mn$ would need $mn=6$, giving only $(35,12,37)$ and $(5,12,13)$.
