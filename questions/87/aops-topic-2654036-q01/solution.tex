Since $10\equiv-1\pmod{11}$, a number with digits $d_k\dots d_1d_0$ (where $d_0$ is the units digit) satisfies
\[d_k\cdot10^k+\cdots+d_1\cdot10+d_0\equiv d_0-d_1+d_2-\cdots\pmod{11},\]
so the alternating sum must start with a plus sign at the \emph{units} digit. For $362880$:
\[0-8+8-2+6-3=1,\]
so the remainder is $\boxed{1}$. Indeed $11\cdot32989=362879$.

The student started with a plus sign at the leftmost digit. With six digits, the leftmost digit is $d_5$, whose sign should be minus, so every sign was flipped and the student obtained $-1$ instead of $1$. Starting from the left only works when the number of digits is odd. (Another check: $362880=9!$, and Wilson's theorem $10!\equiv-1\pmod{11}$ with $10\equiv-1$ gives $9!\equiv1$.)
