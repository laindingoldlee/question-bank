\textbf{The circumcircle of $ABC$.} The angle at $B$ is a right angle, so by Thales' theorem the hypotenuse $AC$ is a diameter of the circumcircle. Its centre is the midpoint of $AC$, which is $O$ (the diagonals of a square bisect each other), and its radius is $\dfrac{AC}{2}=\dfrac{a\sqrt2}{2}$.

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(5cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
real a=2; pair A=(0,0), B=(a,0), C=(a,a), D=(0,a), O=(a/2,a/2);
draw(circle(O,a*sqrt(2)/2),red);
draw(A--B--C--D--cycle,linewidth(0.9));
draw(A--C,blue); draw(B--D,gray+dashed);
draw(rotate(30,O)*(A--B--C--D--cycle),gray+dotted);
dot(O);
label("$A$",A,SW); label("$B$",B,SE); label("$C$",C,NE); label("$D$",D,NW); label("$O$",O,2.2*W);
label("$a$",(A+B)/2,N); label("$\dfrac{a\sqrt{2}}{2}$",(O+C)/2,dir(-45),blue+fontsize(9pt));
label("\mbox{}",(min(currentpicture,user=true)+max(currentpicture,user=true))/2,black); // keeps the text after the figure black when LaTeX typesets the labels inline
\end{asy}

\textbf{The rotated square.} A rotation about $O$ preserves distances from $O$, so during the full turn each vertex stays at distance $OA=OB=OC=OD=\dfrac{a\sqrt2}{2}$ from $O$ and runs once around the circle with centre $O$ and radius $\dfrac{a\sqrt2}{2}$, the circumcircle found above.

Every point of the square is within distance $\dfrac{a\sqrt2}{2}$ of $O$ (the vertices are the farthest points), so the square never leaves this disc, and the vertices reach every point of the circle. Hence the region swept out is exactly the disc, and its boundary is the circumcircle of $ABC$. $\blacksquare$
