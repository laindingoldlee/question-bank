An optimal decomposition of $n\ge6$ has no $1$: merging a $1$ with another part increases the product. No part $k\ge5$ occurs, since replacing it with $3$ and $k-3$ changes its contribution from $k$ to $3k-9>k$. Replace each $4$ by $2+2$ without changing the product. Finally three $2$'s are inferior to two $3$'s, since $8<9$. Hence an optimum uses $3$'s and at most two $2$'s. The sum determines which case occurs:
\[
\boxed{M(n)=
\begin{cases}
3^{\dfrac{n}{3}},&n\equiv0\pmod3,\\
4\cdot3^{\dfrac{n-4}{3}},&n\equiv1\pmod3,\\
2\cdot3^{\dfrac{n-2}{3}},&n\equiv2\pmod3.
\end{cases}}
\]
The indicated decompositions attain these products, so the bound is sharp. In particular $9=3+3+3$ gives $\boxed{27}$; $10=3+3+2+2$ gives $\boxed{36}$; $11=3+3+3+2$ gives $\boxed{54}$.
