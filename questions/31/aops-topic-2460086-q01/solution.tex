\textbf{(a)} Every positive integer can be written uniquely as $n=sk^2$ with $s$ squarefree (not divisible by the square of a prime); $s$ is the product of the primes that occur to an odd power in $n$. Then $ab$ is a perfect square exactly when every prime occurs to an even power in $ab$, i.e. exactly when $a$ and $b$ have the same squarefree part. So we count pairs within each class:
\begin{itemize}
\item $s=1$: $\{1,4,9,16,25,36\}$, giving $\dbinom62=15$ pairs;
\item $s=2$: $\{2,8,18,32\}$, giving $\dbinom42=6$;
\item $s=3$: $\{3,12,27\}$, giving $\dbinom32=3$;
\item $s=5$: $\{5,20\}$; $s=6$: $\{6,24\}$; $s=7$: $\{7,28\}$; one pair each.
\end{itemize}
For every other squarefree $s\le36$ we have $s\ge10$, so $4s>36$ and the class is just $\{s\}$. The total is $15+6+3+1+1+1=\boxed{27}$.

\textbf{(b)} A set has no order: $\{a,b\}$ and $\{b,a\}$ are the \emph{same} subset, so the ``pairing'' matches each subset with itself and proves nothing. It is the \emph{ordered} pairs $(a,b)$ with $a\ne b$ that come in pairs, and there are $2\cdot27=54$ of them.
