The integers that reach $1$ are exactly $3^j$, $2\cdot3^j$, and $4\cdot3^j$ for nonnegative integers $j$. They all work because multiples of three reduce, while $2\mapsto4\mapsto27\mapsto9\mapsto3\mapsto1$. To prove no others work, examine predecessors of this set. Multiples of three merely multiply a target by three. For $n\equiv2\pmod3$, $f(n)=(n-1)(n+2)\equiv1\pmod3$, so a target in the set must be $1,2$, or $4$; only $n=2$ produces one, namely $4$. For $n\equiv1\pmod3$ and $n>1$, $f(n)=(n-1)(n+5)$. If $n$ is odd, these two even factors differ by six, so one is divisible by four and the other by two; their product has at least three factors of two, impossible for the target set. If $n$ is even, both factors are odd and the target must be a power of three. Dividing the factors by three gives two powers of three differing by two, necessarily $1,3$, so $n=4$. This proves the predecessor closure. Within the stated range, the three families contribute six values each: $3^1$ through $3^6$, $2\cdot3^0$ through $2\cdot3^5$, and $4\cdot3^0$ through $4\cdot3^5$. The answer is $\boxed{18}$.
