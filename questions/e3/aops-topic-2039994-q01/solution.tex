\textbf{(a)} Take \(A=\{3,5,9,29\}\). The four three-element sums are
\[
3+5+9=17,\quad 3+5+29=37,\quad 3+9+29=41,\quad 5+9+29=43,
\]
all prime. (Other examples exist.)

\textbf{(b)} Suppose \(A\) has at least \(5\) elements and look at their remainders modulo \(3\).
\begin{itemize}
\item If some remainder occurs at least three times, the sum of those three elements is divisible by \(3\).
\item Otherwise each remainder occurs at most twice, so with at least \(5\) elements all three remainders \(0,1,2\) occur; one element of each remainder gives a sum \(\equiv0+1+2\equiv0\pmod3\).
\end{itemize}
Either way some three elements have a sum divisible by \(3\). That sum is at least \(1+2+3=6>3\), so it is not prime, a contradiction. Hence a prime set has at most \(4\) elements. \(\blacksquare\)
