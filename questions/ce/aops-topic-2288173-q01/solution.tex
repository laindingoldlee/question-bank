The statement is true.

Let $m$ be a multiple of $n$ with base-$(n+1)$ digits $d_k,\dots,d_1,d_0$, so that
\[m=d_k(n+1)^k+\cdots+d_1(n+1)+d_0.\]
Since $n+1\equiv1\pmod n$, every power $(n+1)^i\equiv1\pmod n$, hence
\[m\equiv d_k+\cdots+d_1+d_0\pmod n.\]
As $n\mid m$, the digit sum is divisible by $n$. $\blacksquare$

For $n=9$ this is the familiar rule that the digit sum of a multiple of $9$ (in base $10$) is a multiple of $9$. Another example: $n=4$, $m=28=103_5$, digit sum $4$.
