No. Suppose a balanced four occurs. It has a neighbour one, so put such a one at $(0,0)$. Its four neighbours must all be fours. Each diagonal neighbour of the one sees two fours, so cannot itself be four; it cannot be one either, because its neighbours already include a four and a non-four required by a neighbouring four. More directly, each cardinal four requires its three other neighbours to be 2,3,4, so diagonal neighbours lie in $\{2,3,4\}$, and the repeated neighbouring fours exclude four. Thus the four diagonals alternate two and three, and the cells $(\pm2,0),(0,\pm2)$ are fours. A cell such as $(2,1)$ neighbours a four and a diagonal two or three, so it cannot be one. Hence the four at $(2,0)$ must obtain its required one at $(3,0)$. Similarly ones occur at $(\pm3,0),(0,\pm3)$. Repeating this local implication puts ones at every point of $3\mathbb{Z}^2$, with fours at their cardinal neighbours. Inside each resulting $3\times3$ mesh square, the four remaining cells form a $2\times2$ block of twos and threes, each seeing two boundary fours. A two requires its two internal neighbours to have the same digit; a three requires those two digits to differ. On a four-cycle these conditions allow only all twos: with one three or two opposite threes, a three sees two twos; with two adjacent threes, a two sees unlike neighbours; with three threes, the middle three sees two threes; with four threes, every three does. But the original one required diagonal threes. This contradiction proves that all four digits cannot occur in an everywhere-balanced grid.
