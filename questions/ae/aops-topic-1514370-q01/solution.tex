Wilbur's plane is a scaled copy of Orville's with scale factor $\dfrac{1}{2}$ in every linear dimension.

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(5cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
pair[] plane={(0,0),(0.6,0.15),(2.2,0.15),(3,0.9),(3.4,0.9),(2.9,0.15),(4.2,0.15),(4.6,0.55),(4.85,0.55),(4.7,0),(4.85,-0.55),(4.6,-0.55),(4.2,-0.15),(2.9,-0.15),(3.4,-0.9),(3,-0.9),(2.2,-0.15),(0.6,-0.15)};
path pl=plane[0];
for(int i=1;i<plane.length;++i) pl=pl--plane[i];
pl=pl--cycle;
pl=rotate(90)*pl;
fill(pl,rgb(0.8,0.9,1));draw(pl,linewidth(0.9));
path w=shift(3.2,0.6)*scale(0.5)*pl;
fill(w,rgb(1,0.88,0.75));draw(w,linewidth(0.9));
draw((-1.3,0)--(-1.3,4.85),Arrows(TeXHead));label("$L$",(-1.3,2.4),W);
draw((2.2,0.6)--(2.2,0.6+2.425),Arrows(TeXHead));label("$\dfrac {L}{2}$",(2.2,0.6+1.2),W);
label("Orville",(0,-0.3),S);label("Wilbur",(3.2,0.3),S);
label("\mbox{}",(min(currentpicture,user=true)+max(currentpicture,user=true))/2,black); // keeps the text after the figure black when LaTeX typesets the labels inline
\end{asy}

Areas scale by the square of the linear factor, so Wilbur's wing area is $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^2=\dfrac{1}{4}$ of Orville's. Since the lift force is proportional to the wing area, the ratio of the lift forces is $\boxed{\dfrac{1}{4}}$.
