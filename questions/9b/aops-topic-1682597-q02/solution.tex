\textbf{Answer: exactly the values $6<S\le10$.} (The poster's answer ``$S=8$ always'' only covers cuts by one horizontal and one vertical line through the whole square.)

\textbf{A formula.} Let the rectangles be $w_i\times h_i$. The perimeters together run once along the boundary of the square (length $4$) and twice along every interior cut (once for the rectangle on each side). So $S=4+2L$, where $L$ is the total length of the interior cuts.

\textbf{Upper bound.} Since $0<w_i,h_i\le1$, $(1-w_i)(1-h_i)\ge0$, i.e.\ $w_i+h_i\le1+w_ih_i$. Adding over the four rectangles and using $\sum w_ih_i=1$,
\[
S=2\sum(w_i+h_i)\le2(4+1)=10 .
\]

\textbf{Lower bound.} Suppose some vertical line $x=t$ (avoiding the finitely many $x$-coordinates of rectangle corners) crosses no horizontal cut. Then it runs from bottom to top inside a single rectangle, which therefore has height $1$; that rectangle is not the whole square, so one of its vertical sides is an interior cut of length $1$. Otherwise every such vertical line crosses a horizontal cut, so the horizontal cuts project onto the whole bottom side and have total length at least $1$. Either way the cuts in one direction have total length at least $1$, so $L\ge1$. If $L=1$, there are no cuts at all in the other direction; but with, say, no vertical cuts every rectangle spans the full width, the rectangles are four horizontal strips, and $L=3$. So $L>1$ and $S>6$.

\textbf{All these values occur.}

Four parallel strips give $L=3$, $S=10$. Cut off a full-height strip of width $1-w$ and divide the remaining $w\times1$ strip into three by two horizontal cuts of length $w$: then $L=1+2w$ and $S=6+4w$, which takes every value in $6<S<10$ as $w$ runs over $0<w<1$ (for instance $w=\dfrac{1}{2}$ gives $S=8$, as do the cuts in the middle picture). Hence the possible values of $S$ are exactly $\boxed{6<S\le10}$.
