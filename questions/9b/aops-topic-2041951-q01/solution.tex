\textbf{(a)} The partial sums are \(1+2+\cdots+n=\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\), which exceed any given real number for large \(n\). So the partial sums do not approach a real limit, and \(S\) is not a real number (the series diverges to \(+\infty\)).

\textbf{(b)} The rules ``add two series term by term'', ``shift a series and add'', and ``a series equals the sum of its terms'' are theorems only for \emph{convergent} series. None of \(S_1\), \(S_2\), \(S\) converges (their terms do not even tend to \(0\)).
\begin{itemize}
\item Step 1 \emph{assigns} the value \(\dfrac{1}{2}\) by a different rule (Cesaro averaging of partial sums); that is a definition of a generalised sum, not the ordinary sum.
\item Step 2 shifts and adds a divergent series, and step 3 subtracts the divergent \(S_2\) from the divergent \(S\) while also treating \(S\) as a number, so that \(S-S_2=4S\) can be rearranged as \(3S=-S_2\). By part (a), \(S\) is not a number, so this algebra is meaningless. Moreover, the term-by-term pairing \(0+4+0+8+\cdots=4(1+2+3+\cdots)\) silently deletes the zeros and spreads the terms out, which changes divergent series.
\end{itemize}
(The value \(-\dfrac{1}{12}\) does arise in advanced mathematics as the value \(\zeta(-1)\) of the analytically continued zeta function, or from other regularisation methods, but it is not the sum of the series in the ordinary sense, and Cesaro summation does not give it.)

\textbf{(c)} Treat \(T=1+1+1+\cdots\) as a number and allow shifting. Then
\[
T=1+(1+1+1+\cdots)=1+T,
\]
so \(0=1\). Similarly, inserting a zero, \(S=0+1+2+3+\cdots\); subtracting term by term from \(S=1+2+3+\cdots\) gives \(0=S-S=1+1+1+\cdots=T\), contradicting \(T=1+T\) again. These contradictions show that the rules used in the post cannot be applied to divergent series. \(\blacksquare\)
