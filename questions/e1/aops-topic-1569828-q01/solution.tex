\textbf{(a)} Suppose $s$ were the smallest positive real number. Then $\dfrac {s}{2}$ is a real number with $0<\dfrac {s}{2}<s$, a positive real number smaller than $s$, a contradiction. So there is no smallest positive real number. $\blacksquare$

\textbf{(b)} Let $\varepsilon>0$ and choose an integer $N\ge\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}$ (possible because the integers are unbounded). For $n>N$ we have $n>\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}$, so $\dfrac{1}{n}<\varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$

\textbf{What this says about $\dfrac{1}{\infty}$.} Infinity is not a real number, so $\dfrac{1}{\infty}$ is not an ordinary quotient, and by (a) it cannot mean ``the smallest positive number'', since there is none. What \emph{is} true is statement (b): $\dfrac{1}{n}$ becomes smaller than any positive number you name once $n$ is large enough. This is what it means to say that $\dfrac{1}{n}$ tends to $0$ as $n\to\infty$, and ``$\dfrac{1}{\infty}=0$'' is shorthand for this limit. In the same sense $\dfrac{2}{n}$ also tends to $0$, so ``$\dfrac{1}{\infty}$'' and ``$\dfrac{2}{\infty}$'' are both $0$. Expressions such as $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$ have no value: for example $\dfrac{n}{n}\to1$ but $\dfrac{2n}{n}\to2$, although both look like ``$\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$''. Similarly, division by $0$ is undefined, so $\dfrac{\infty}{0}$ is meaningless.
