Since $CA=CB$, we have $CE=\dfrac12CB=\dfrac12CA=CF$, and $\angle ECF=90^\circ$, so the square $CEDF$ with sides $CE$ and $CF$ exists; $D$ is its fourth vertex, with $ED\perp CB$ and $ED=CE$.

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(4cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
pair C=(0,0), B=(4,0), A=(0,4), Ep=(2,0), F=(0,2), D=(2,2);
fill(C--Ep--D--F--cycle,rgb(0.85,0.9,1));
draw(A--B--C--cycle,linewidth(0.9)); draw(Ep--D--F,blue);
draw((0.25,0)--(0.25,0.25)--(0,0.25));
draw((1,-0.12)--(1,0.12)); draw((3,-0.12)--(3,0.12)); draw((-0.12,1)--(0.12,1)); draw((-0.12,3)--(0.12,3));
dot(Ep); dot(F); dot(D);
label("$C$",C,SW); label("$B$",B,SE); label("$A$",A,NW); label("$E$",Ep,S); label("$F$",F,W); label("$D$",D,NE);
\end{asy}

Let $M$ be the midpoint of $AB$. By the midpoint theorem in triangle $ABC$, the segment $EM$ joining the midpoints of $CB$ and $AB$ is parallel to $CA$ and has length $\dfrac12CA$. Since $CA\perp CB$, the segment $EM$ is perpendicular to $CB$ at $E$, on the same side of $CB$ as $A$, with length $\dfrac12CA=CE$.

The point $D$ has exactly the same description: $ED\perp CB$, $D$ is on the same side of $CB$ as $F$ (and hence as $A$), and $ED=CE$. There is only one such point, so $D=M$. Hence $D$ is the midpoint of $AB$, and in particular it lies on $AB$. $\blacksquare$

(With coordinates $C=(0,0)$, $B=(2,0)$, $A=(0,2)$: $E=(1,0)$, $F=(0,1)$, $D=(1,1)$, which lies on $x+y=2$, the line $AB$.)
