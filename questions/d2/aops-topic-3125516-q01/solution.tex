Assume $a\le b$. If $a=b$, division by $2$ gives $c^a=a!$. For $a=2$ this is impossible; for $a\ge3$, the right side has prime factors $2$ and $3$, so cannot be a power of one prime. Suppose $a<b$. If $a=2$, reduction modulo $c$ shows that either $c>b$ or $c=2$, since otherwise $c\mid b!$ forces $c\mid2$. For $c>b$, $c^b>b!+2$, impossible. For $c=2$, $b=3$ gives $12\ne8$, while $b\ge5$ gives left side divisible by $4$ and right side $2$ modulo $4$. Finally suppose $a,b$ are distinct odd primes. If $c=2$, then $c^a+c^b$ is divisible by $2^a$, whereas $a!+b!=a!(1+(a+1)\cdots b)$ has the same power of $2$ dividing it as $a!$, strictly less than $a$. If $c$ is odd, reducing modulo $3$ forces $c=3$. Then $3^a+3^b=3^a(1+3^{b-a})$ has exactly one factor of $2$, as $b-a$ is even. Consequently $a!$ has exactly one factor of $2$, forcing $a=3$. For $b=5$ the two sides are $270$ and $126$, unequal. For $b\ge7$, the equation becomes $b!=21+3^b$, impossible modulo $9$. Thus there are no prime triples.
