Every square with vertices at the dots fits snugly in an axis-parallel bounding square of side \(n\) (\(1\le n\le 4\)). Conversely, inside a bounding square of side \(n\) there are exactly \(n\) inscribed squares with vertices at dots. Starting from one corner of the bounding square, go \(k\) units along each side (\(k=0,1,\dots,n-1\)). The four points so obtained form a square with side vector \((n-k,k)\); the case \(k=0\) is the bounding square itself.

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(4cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
for(int i=0;i<5;++i) for(int j=0;j<5;++j) dot((i,j));
draw((1,0)--(4,1)--(3,4)--(0,3)--cycle,blue+linewidth(0.9));
draw((1,0)--(4,0)--(4,1),red+dashed); 
label("$3$",(2.5,0),S,red); label("$1$",(4,0.5),E,red);
label("\mbox{}",(min(currentpicture,user=true)+max(currentpicture,user=true))/2,black); // keeps the text after the figure black when LaTeX typesets the labels inline
\end{asy}

(The figure shows the case \(n=4\), \(k=1\).) Different pairs (bounding square, \(k\)) give different squares, since a square determines its bounding box and the offset \(k\). There are \((5-n)^2\) bounding squares of side \(n\), so the total is
\[
\sum_{n=1}^{4}n(5-n)^2=1\cdot 16+2\cdot 9+3\cdot 4+4\cdot 1=16+18+12+4=\boxed{50}.
\]
The poster's \(16+9+4+1=30\) counts only the upright squares; the other \(20\) are tilted.
