A base-$10$ number is a multiple of $3$ exactly when its digit sum is. So we need the digit sums of $n$ in bases $2$, $4$ and $8$ all to be multiples of $3$.

\textbf{Base $4$.} Since $4\equiv1\pmod3$, a number is congruent mod $3$ to its base-$4$ digit sum. So this condition follows from $3\mid n$.

\textbf{Base $2$.} The digit sum is the number of $1$s in binary, which must be $3$ or $6$ (for $n<256$).

\textbf{Base $8$.} The octal digit sum must be a multiple of $3$.

Check the multiples of $3$ from $102$ on. Those with $3$ or $6$ binary ones up to $159$ are
\[
111=1101111_2,\ 123=1111011_2,\ 126=1111110_2,\ 138=10001010_2,\ 159=10011111_2,
\]
with octal forms $157_8, 173_8, 176_8, 212_8, 237_8$ and octal digit sums $13,11,14,5,12$. Only $159$ works: $159=10011111_2=2133_4=237_8$, and $10011111$, $2133$, $237$ have digit sums $6$, $9$, $12$. The answer is $\boxed{159}$.
