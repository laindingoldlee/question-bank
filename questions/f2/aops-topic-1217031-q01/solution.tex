Call a diameter \emph{good} if neither of its endpoints is one of the marked points. All but finitely many diameters are good. For a good diameter $d$, with a chosen side called ``left'', let $f(d)$ be the number of marked points on its left.

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(3cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
draw(circle((0,0),1));
real[] t={10,35,62,100,128,150,200,230,248,275,300,330,345};
for(real a: t) dot(dir(a));
draw(dir(80)--dir(260),blue+linewidth(0.8)); label("$d$",dir(80),N);
label("\mbox{}",(min(currentpicture,user=true)+max(currentpicture,user=true))/2,black); // keeps the text after the figure black when LaTeX typesets the labels inline
\end{asy}

Start with a good diameter $d_0$ and rotate it continuously by $180^\circ$. At the end it is the same line with left and right exchanged, so the count goes from $k=f(d_0)$ to $888-k$.

While the diameter rotates, $f$ changes only when an endpoint passes a marked point. If one endpoint passes a marked point, that point switches sides and $f$ changes by exactly $1$. If both endpoints pass marked points at once, these two points are diametrically opposite; one moves from left to right and the other from right to left, so $f$ does not change. Hence $f$ moves in steps of $0$ or $\pm1$, always taking its values at good positions.

The values $k$ and $888-k$ lie on opposite sides of $444$ (or one of them equals $444$), so on the way $f$ takes the value $444$ at some good diameter. That diameter has $444$ points on each side and none on it.
