Suppose, for contradiction, that $a^3+b^3=m^2$ for an integer $m$ and that $s=a+b=pq$ with distinct primes $p$ and $q$. Factor
\[a^3+b^3=(a+b)\left(a^2-ab+b^2\right)=s\left(s^2-3ab\right),\]
using $a^2-ab+b^2=(a+b)^2-3ab$.

\textbf{Each prime factor $r$ of $s$ divides $3ab$.} Let $r\in\{p,q\}$. Since $s=pq$ with $p\ne q$, the prime $r$ appears exactly once in $s$. In the square $m^2$ every prime appears an even number of times, so $r$ must also divide the second factor $s^2-3ab$. As $r\mid s$, this gives $r\mid 3ab$.

\textbf{If $r\ne3$, then $r$ divides both $a$ and $b$.} Indeed $r\mid ab$, so $r$ divides $a$ or $b$; since $r$ also divides $a+b$, it divides the other one too.

\textbf{Case 1: neither $p$ nor $q$ is $3$.} Then $p$ and $q$ both divide $a$ and $b$, so $pq\mid a$ and $pq\mid b$. Hence $a\ge pq$ and $b\ge pq$, and $a+b\ge2pq>pq$, a contradiction.

\textbf{Case 2: one of the primes is $3$.} Since $p\ne q$, the other one, call it $r$, is not $3$, so $r\mid a$ and $r\mid b$. Write $a=ra'$ and $b=rb'$. Then $a'+b'=\dfrac{a+b}{r}=3$, so $\{a',b'\}=\{1,2\}$, and
\[a^3+b^3=r^3\left(1^3+2^3\right)=9r^3=m^2 .\]
Then $r^3=\left(\dfrac {m}{3}\right)^2$ is the square of a rational number, hence of an integer, but the prime $r$ appears an odd number of times in $r^3$. Contradiction.

So $a+b$ is never the product of two distinct primes. $\blacksquare$

\emph{Remark.} The word ``distinct'' matters for the argument but the statement is sharp in other ways: $1^3+2^3=3^2$ with $a+b=3$ prime, and $2^3+2^3=4^2$ with $a+b=4=2\cdot2$.
