\textbf{(a)} Suppose $\sqrt2=\dfrac {b}{a}$ with positive integers $a,b$. Then $b^2=2a^2$. In the prime factorisation of a square, every prime appears an even number of times; so $2$ appears an even number of times in $b^2$, and an odd number of times in $2a^2$ (once more than in $a^2$). This contradicts the uniqueness of prime factorisation. $\blacksquare$

\textbf{(b)} Suppose $\sqrt2=\dfrac {p}{q}$ with positive integers $p,q$, and choose such a representation with $q$ as small as possible. Since $1<\sqrt2<2$, we have $q<p<2q$, so $p-q$ and $2q-p$ are positive integers and $p-q<q$. Using $p=\sqrt2\,q$,
\[
\dfrac{2q-p}{p-q}=\dfrac{\left(2-\sqrt2\right)q}{\left(\sqrt2-1\right)q}=\dfrac{\sqrt2\left(\sqrt2-1\right)}{\sqrt2-1}=\sqrt2 .
\]
So $\sqrt2=\dfrac{2q-p}{p-q}$ is a representation with the smaller positive denominator $p-q<q$, contradicting the choice of $q$. Hence $\sqrt2$ is irrational. $\blacksquare$
