Let $O$ be the intersection of the diagonals $AC$ and $BD$; it lies inside $ABCD$ because the quadrilateral is convex.

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(5cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
pair A=(0,0),B=(5,-1),C=(6,3.2),D=(1,4);
pair O=extension(A,C,B,D);
pair M=(2.3,0.9);
draw(A--B--C--D--cycle,linewidth(1));
draw(A--C);draw(B--D);
draw(M--A,dashed+gray);draw(M--B,dashed+gray);draw(M--C,dashed+gray);draw(M--D,dashed+gray);
dot(O,red);dot(M);
label("$A$",A,SW);label("$B$",B,SE);label("$C$",C,NE);label("$D$",D,NW);
label("$O$",O,N,red);label("$M$",M,S);
label("\mbox{}",(min(currentpicture,user=true)+max(currentpicture,user=true))/2,black); // keeps the text after the figure black when LaTeX typesets the labels inline
\end{asy}

For any point $M$, the triangle inequality gives
\[
MA+MC\ge AC\qquad\text{and}\qquad MB+MD\ge BD,
\]
with equality in the first exactly when $M$ is on segment $AC$, and in the second exactly when $M$ is on segment $BD$. Adding,
\[
MA+MB+MC+MD\ge AC+BD,
\]
and equality holds exactly when $M$ lies on both diagonals, i.e.\ $M=O$. For $M=O$ the sum is $AC+BD$.

Hence the minimum is attained at a unique point: $\boxed{M\text{ is the intersection point of the diagonals } AC \text{ and } BD}$.
