\textbf{(a)} The $6$ people fill $6$ distinguishable places ($A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, first in line, second in line), so there are $6!=\boxed{720}$ arrangements.

\textbf{(b)} Only the people in $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ matter, in order: $10\cdot9\cdot8\cdot7=\boxed{5040}$ arrangements. (The remaining six people form an unordered group.)

\textbf{(c)} Frank is chosen exactly when the person who gets out is one of his two best friends. Each of the other $9$ people is equally likely to be in any of the $4$ positions or in the line, so a given best friend is in one of the positions with probability $\dfrac{4}{9}$; and then he is the one to get out with probability $\dfrac{1}{4}$. So each best friend gets out next with probability $\dfrac{4}{9}\cdot\dfrac{1}{4}=\dfrac{1}{9}$. These two events cannot both happen, so the probability that Frank is chosen is
\[\dfrac{1}{9}+\dfrac{1}{9}=\boxed{\dfrac{2}{9}}.\]
(Equivalently: by symmetry the person who gets out next is equally likely to be any of the $9$ people other than Frank, and $2$ of them choose Frank.)
