\textbf{Areas in terms of $P$.} A circle of circumference $P$ has radius $\dfrac{P}{2\pi}$ and area $\pi\left(\dfrac{P}{2\pi}\right)^2=\dfrac{P^2}{4\pi}$.

A regular $n$-gon ($n\ge3$) of perimeter $P$ has side $\dfrac {P}{n}$. Joining its centre to the vertices cuts it into $n$ congruent isosceles triangles with apex angle $\dfrac{2\pi}{n}$; the height of each from the centre (the apothem) is $\dfrac{P}{2n}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\tan\dfrac{\pi} {n}}$. So the area is
\[
n\cdot\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac {P}{n}\cdot\dfrac{P}{2n\tan\dfrac{\pi} {n}}=\dfrac{P^2}{4n\tan\dfrac{\pi} {n}}.
\]

\textbf{Comparison.} The circle has the larger area exactly when $n\tan\dfrac{\pi} {n}>\pi$, i.e.\ when $\tan t>t$ for $t=\dfrac{\pi} {n}\in\left(0,\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right]$. This holds for every $0<t<\dfrac{\pi}{2}$: in a unit circle with centre $O$, let the radius $OA$ and the ray at angle $t$ from it be drawn, and let the tangent at $A$ meet that ray at $T$. The sector $OA$ of angle $t$ (area $\dfrac {t}{2}$) lies inside the right triangle $OAT$ (area $\dfrac{\tan t}{2}$) and is strictly smaller, so $t<\tan t$. $\blacksquare$

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(9cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
real P=2*pi;
draw(circle((0,0),1),linewidth(0.9));
pair O2=(3.0,0); real R6=P/6;
path hx=O2+R6*dir(0);
for(int i=1;i<6;++i) hx=hx--(O2+R6*dir(60*i));
hx=hx--cycle;
draw(hx,blue+linewidth(0.9));
pair O3=(5.9,-0.15); real s3=P/3; real R3=s3/sqrt(3);
path tr=(O3+R3*dir(90))--(O3+R3*dir(210))--(O3+R3*dir(330))--cycle;
draw(tr,red+linewidth(0.9));
label("circle",(0,-1),S);label("hexagon",O2+(0,-R6*sqrt(3)/2),S,blue);label("triangle",O3+(0,-R3/2),S,red);
label("\mbox{}",(min(currentpicture,user=true)+max(currentpicture,user=true))/2,black); // keeps the text after the figure black when LaTeX typesets the labels inline
\end{asy}

(The figure shows a circle, a regular hexagon and an equilateral triangle with the same perimeter $2\pi$, to scale. Their areas are $\pi\approx3.14$, about $2.85$ and about $1.90$.)

The poster's general question, whether the circle beats \emph{every} closed curve of the same perimeter, is the isoperimetric inequality $4\pi A\le P^2$. It is true, but its proof is much harder than the comparison with regular polygons proved here.
