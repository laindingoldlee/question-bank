The statement is \textbf{true}.

\textbf{Fact.} For a positive integer the digital root is the number in $\{1,2,\dots,9\}$ that is congruent to the integer modulo $9$. Indeed, $10\equiv1\pmod 9$, so $10^k\equiv1$ and every number is congruent to its digit sum modulo $9$. Each step of the process therefore keeps the remainder modulo $9$, and keeps the number positive (a positive integer has a nonzero digit). The process ends with a single digit from $1$ to $9$, which is determined by the remainder: remainder $r\ne0$ gives $r$, and remainder $0$ gives $9$.

\textbf{Proof of the claim.} Let the number have digits whose sum is $s$, one of which is a non-leading $9$. Leaving the $9$ out gives the digit sum $s-9$. Since the $9$ is not the leading digit, the leading digit (which is at least $1$) is still counted, so $s-9\ge1$; thus both $s$ and $s-9$ are positive integers. They are congruent modulo $9$, so by the fact their digital roots are equal. $\blacksquare$

(The condition that the $9$ is not the leading digit is only needed to keep something positive: for the number $9$ itself, leaving out the $9$ leaves nothing. For a number such as $90$ or $900$, leaving out the leading $9$ gives the sum $0$, which is not a valid digital root.)
