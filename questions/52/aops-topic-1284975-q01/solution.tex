\textbf{(a)} Let the pair be $q$ and $q+2$ with $q\ne3$; then $q\ge5$, and the average is $q+1$. Since $q$ is an odd prime, $q+1$ is even. Of the three consecutive integers $q,q+1,q+2$, one is divisible by $3$; the primes $q$ and $q+2$ are greater than $3$, so neither is divisible by $3$, and therefore $3\mid q+1$. Being divisible by $2$ and by $3$, the average $q+1$ is a multiple of $6$. (The pair $3,5$ has average $4$.)

\textbf{(b)} Since $6\equiv1\pmod5$, we have $6p\equiv p\pmod5$.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $p\equiv1\pmod5$, then $6p-1\equiv0\pmod5$, and $6p-1>5$, so $6p-1$ is composite.
\item If $p\equiv4\pmod5$, then $6p+1\equiv5\equiv0\pmod5$, and $6p+1>5$, so $6p+1$ is composite.
\end{itemize}
In both cases $6p\pm1$ are not both prime.

\textbf{(c)} Check the primes in order:
\[p=2:\ 11,13;\qquad p=3:\ 17,19;\qquad p=5:\ 29,31;\qquad p=7:\ 41,43,\]
all prime, but for $p=11$ we get $65=5\cdot13$ and $67$. The answer is $\boxed{11}$, as (b) predicts, since $11\equiv1\pmod5$.

\emph{Remark.} By (b) the trick fails for every prime that is $1$ or $4$ more than a multiple of $5$, such as $11$, $19$, $29$, $31$, $41$, $59$, $61$, and so on, which is roughly half of all primes; it is far from ``extremely accurate''.
