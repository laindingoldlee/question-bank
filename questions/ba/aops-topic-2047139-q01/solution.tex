Number the cards in each deck \(1,2,\dots,12\) in dealing order. Jane deals her \(i\)-th card at time \(2i-1\) and Bob deals his \(j\)-th card at time \(2j\).

Let \(J\) be the position of Jane's \emph{last} red card and \(B\) the position of Bob's \emph{first} red card. Jane finishes her reds before Bob's first red exactly when \(2J-1<2B\), that is, when \(J\le B\).

The two decks are shuffled independently, and in each deck the three red positions form a uniformly random \(3\)-element subset of \(\{1,\dots,12\}\). Hence
\[
P(J=t)=\dfrac{\binom{t-1}{2}}{\binom{12}{3}},\qquad P(B\ge t)=\dfrac{\binom{13-t}{3}}{\binom{12}{3}},
\]
since \(J=t\) means one red at position \(t\) and two among the first \(t-1\), and \(B\ge t\) means all three reds among positions \(t,\dots,12\). Therefore, with \(\binom{12}{3}=220\),
\[
P(J\le B)=\sum_{t=3}^{10}\dfrac{\binom{t-1}{2}\binom{13-t}{3}}{220^2}.
\]
The terms \(\binom{t-1}{2}\binom{13-t}{3}\) for \(t=3,4,\dots,10\) are
\[
1\cdot120,\ 3\cdot84,\ 6\cdot56,\ 10\cdot35,\ 15\cdot20,\ 21\cdot10,\ 28\cdot4,\ 36\cdot1,
\]
that is \(120+252+336+350+300+210+112+36=1716\). So the probability is
\[
\dfrac{1716}{48400}=\boxed{\dfrac{39}{1100}}.
\]
(In the \(0\)-\(1\) language of the official solution: write each deck as a string with \(1\) for red and \(0\) for other; the condition only compares the last \(1\) of Jane's string with the first \(1\) of Bob's.)
