The roots of $x^2-x-1$ are $\varphi=\dfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$ and $\psi=\dfrac{1-\sqrt5}{2}$. Both are irrational, since $\sqrt5$ is irrational.

\textbf{(a)} Divide by $x^2-x-1$:
\[
ax^2+bx+c=a(x^2-x-1)+(a+b)x+(a+c).
\]
Let $\alpha\in\{\varphi,\psi\}$ be the common root. Putting $x=\alpha$ gives $(a+b)\alpha+(a+c)=0$. If $a+b\ne0$, then $\alpha=-\dfrac{a+c}{a+b}$ would be rational, a contradiction. So $a+b=0$, and then $a+c=0$ as well. Hence $b=-a$, $c=-a$ and
\[
ax^2+bx+c=a(x^2-x-1),
\]
a constant multiple. \textbf{Yes} for rational coefficients.

\textbf{(b)} With real coefficients the answer is \textbf{no}: for example
\[
x(x-\varphi)=x^2-\dfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\,x
\]
has the root $\varphi$ in common with $x^2-x-1$, but its other root is $0$, which is not a root of $x^2-x-1$, so it is not a multiple of $x^2-x-1$.
