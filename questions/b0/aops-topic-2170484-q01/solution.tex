\textbf{(a)} For $n=1,2,3,4$ we get $7$, $13$, $19$, $25$. The first three are prime and $25=5^2$ is not, so the answer is $n=\boxed{4}$.

\textbf{(b)} Take $n=5k+4$ with $k\ge0$. Then
\[6n+1=30k+25=5(6k+5),\]
and $6k+5\ge5>1$, so $6n+1$ is composite. This happens for infinitely many $n$ ($n=4,9,14,\dots$). (Similarly $n=7k+1$ gives $6n+1=7(6k+1)$, composite for $k\ge1$.)

\textbf{(c)} Every integer $m$ can be written as $6n$, $6n\pm1$, $6n\pm2$ or $6n+3$. The numbers $6n$ and $6n\pm2$ are even, and $6n+3=3(2n+1)$ is a multiple of $3$. A prime $p>3$ is neither even nor a multiple of $3$, so $p=6n+1$ or $p=6n-1$ (with $n\ge1$, since $p\ge5$).

So all primes except $2$ and $3$ lie in the two progressions $6n+1$ and $6n-1$, which together contain only one third of all integers. A number $6n+1$ is automatically divisible by neither $2$ nor $3$, so it is prime much more often than a typical number of the same size: among $n=1,\dots,10$ the values $7,13,19,25,31,37,43,49,55,61$ include seven primes. It is not a coincidence, but it is not a rule either: by (b) there are infinitely many exceptions, beginning with $25$, $49$ and $55$.
