Write $h_n=\dfrac{1}{n}$, $t_n$ and $g_n$ for the $n$th terms of $H$, $T$ and $G$, and $H_N,T_N,G_N$ for the sums of the first $N$ terms. All terms are positive, so each sequence of partial sums is increasing and has a limit that is either a finite number or $+\infty$; this limit is the sum of the series.

\textbf{(a)} If $a_n\le b_n$ for every $n$, then $A_N\le B_N$ for every $N$, and letting $N\to\infty$ gives $A\le B$, where either side may be $+\infty$. So both comparisons are correct: $T\le H$ and $G\ge H$. Grouping does not change the sum of a positive series: the $k$th partial sum of $G$ is a partial sum of $T$, namely
\[G_k=1+\dfrac{k-1}{2}=T_{2^{k-1}},\]
and since the partial sums of $T$ increase, both sequences have the same limit. So $T=G$, and the two comparisons say only that
\[T\le H\le G=T,\]
that is, all three sums are equal. There is no contradiction, because all three are $+\infty$: the comparisons read $\infty\le\infty\le\infty$.

What makes the objection look paradoxical is that grouping changes which terms ``correspond''. The $k$th term of $G$ is a whole block of $2^{k-2}$ terms of $T$ (for $k\ge2$), so in terms of partial sums the two comparisons are
\[H_k\le G_k=T_{2^{k-1}}\le H_{2^{k-1}},\]
which compare $H$ with itself at very different lengths.

\textbf{(b)} Suppose $H$ were a finite number. Every $t_n\le h_n$, and $t_3=\dfrac{1}{4}<\dfrac{1}{3}=h_3$, so for $N\ge3$
\[T_N\le H_N-\left(\dfrac{1}{3}-\dfrac{1}{4}\right)=H_N-\dfrac{1}{12}\le H-\dfrac{1}{12}.\]
Letting $N\to\infty$ gives $T\le H-\dfrac{1}{12}$; in particular $T$ is finite. On the other hand $g_1=1=h_1$ and $g_k=\dfrac{1}{2}\ge\dfrac{1}{k}=h_k$ for $k\ge2$, so $G\ge H$, and $G=T$ as in (a). Hence
\[H\le G=T\le H-\dfrac{1}{12},\]
which is impossible. So $H$ cannot be finite: the student's two comparisons together give another proof that $\boxed{\text{the harmonic series diverges}}$.
