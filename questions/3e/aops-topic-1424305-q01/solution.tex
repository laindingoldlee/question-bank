\textbf{$a+b$ and $a$.} Any common divisor $d$ of $a+b$ and $a$ divides $(a+b)-a=b$, so $d$ divides both $a$ and $b$. Since $\gcd(a,b)=1$, $d=1$. Hence $\gcd(a+b,a)=1$, and by the same argument $\gcd(a+b,b)=1$.

\textbf{$a+b$ and $ab$.} Suppose a prime $p$ divides both $a+b$ and $ab$. Since $p\mid ab$, $p$ divides $a$ or $b$; say $p\mid a$. Then $p$ is a common divisor of $a+b$ and $a$, which contradicts $\gcd(a+b,a)=1$. The case $p\mid b$ is the same. So no prime divides both, and $\gcd(a+b,ab)=1$.

(For example, $2$ and $7$ are relatively prime, and $9$ is relatively prime to $2$, $7$ and $14$.)
