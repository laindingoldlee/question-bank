If all four roots are rational, so is their sum. For the converse we use a standard fact about adjoining one square root.

\textbf{Lemma.} Let $K$ be a set of real numbers containing $\mathbb Q$ and closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication and division by nonzero elements (a field). Let $w\in K$, $w>0$, with $\sqrt w\notin K$. Then every number in
\[
K(\sqrt w)=\{u+v\sqrt w:\ u,v\in K\}
\]
has exactly one such representation, $K(\sqrt w)$ is again a field, and the "conjugation" $\sigma(u+v\sqrt w)=u-v\sqrt w$ satisfies $\sigma(st)=\sigma(s)\sigma(t)$ and $\sigma(s+t)=\sigma(s)+\sigma(t)$ and fixes every element of $K$.

\emph{Proof of the lemma.} If $u+v\sqrt w=u'+v'\sqrt w$ with $v\ne v'$, then $\sqrt w=\dfrac{u-u'}{v'-v}\in K$, a contradiction; so representations are unique. Sums and products of such numbers have the same form (using $(\sqrt w)^2=w\in K$), and $\dfrac{1}{u+v\sqrt w}=\dfrac{u-v\sqrt w}{u^2-v^2w}$ with $u^2-v^2w\ne0$ (otherwise $\sqrt w=\pm\dfrac {u}{v}\in K$). The rules for $\sigma$ follow by expanding, exactly as for complex conjugation. $\square$

\textbf{Main proof.} Let $q=\sqrt x+\sqrt y+\sqrt z+\sqrt t$ be rational. Start with $L_0=\mathbb Q$ and adjoin the four roots one at a time: $L_1=L_0(\sqrt x)$ if $\sqrt x\notin L_0$ and $L_1=L_0$ otherwise; then $L_2$ from $L_1$ with $\sqrt y$, $L_3$ with $\sqrt z$, $L_4$ with $\sqrt t$. Each $L_i$ is a field of real numbers, and $L_4$ contains all four roots.

Suppose some root is irrational. Then at least one step is a genuine extension; let the \emph{last} genuine step be $L_j=L_{j-1}(\sqrt w)$, where $w$ is one of $x,y,z,t$ and $\sqrt w\notin L_{j-1}$. After it nothing new is added, so $L_4=L_j$. Let $\sigma$ be the conjugation of $L_4$ over $L_{j-1}$ from the lemma.

For each root $r\in\{\sqrt x,\sqrt y,\sqrt z,\sqrt t\}$ we have $r^2\in\mathbb Q\subseteq L_{j-1}$, so
\[
\sigma(r)^2=\sigma(r^2)=r^2,\qquad\text{hence}\qquad \sigma(r)=\pm r .
\]
Moreover $\sigma(\sqrt w)=-\sqrt w$. Applying $\sigma$ to $q=\sqrt x+\sqrt y+\sqrt z+\sqrt t$, and using that $\sigma$ fixes the rational number $q$,
\[
q=\varepsilon_1\sqrt x+\varepsilon_2\sqrt y+\varepsilon_3\sqrt z+\varepsilon_4\sqrt t\qquad(\varepsilon_i=\pm1),
\]
with the sign in front of $\sqrt w$ equal to $-1$. Subtracting this from the original equation,
\[
(1-\varepsilon_1)\sqrt x+(1-\varepsilon_2)\sqrt y+(1-\varepsilon_3)\sqrt z+(1-\varepsilon_4)\sqrt t=0 .
\]
Every term is $\ge0$, and the term with $\sqrt w$ equals $2\sqrt w>0$. This is impossible. Hence all four roots are rational. $\blacksquare$

(The same argument works for any number of square roots of positive rationals. Positivity matters: $\sqrt2+(-\sqrt2)$ is rational.)
