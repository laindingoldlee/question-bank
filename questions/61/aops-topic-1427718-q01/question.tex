Call a positive integer $k>1$ \emph{root-simple} if $\sqrt[n]{k}$ cannot be simplified for any integer $n\ge2$, that is, $k$ has no factor $m^n$ with $m>1$ and $n\ge2$. (Equivalently, $k$ is not divisible by the square of any prime.) Prove that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] there are infinitely many root-simple integers;
\item[(b)] there are infinitely many composite root-simple integers;
\item[(c)] there are infinitely many pairs $(a,b)$ of root-simple integers with $\gcd(a,b)=1$.
\end{enumerate}
(A student proves (a) by saying: take a prime $z$; then $z!$ is divisible by every prime below $z$, and $z!+1$ is "not divisible by every prime under $z$", so it is prime or has a new prime factor. Repair this argument.)
