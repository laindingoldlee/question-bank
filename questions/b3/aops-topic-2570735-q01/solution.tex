Each participant can play at most $8$ others (everyone except themselves and their classmate). So the $9$ different numbers are exactly $0,1,\dots,8$. Call the participant with $k$ games $P_k$, and let $Q$ be the participant from class $A$ who made the observation.

$P_8$ played everyone except his classmate. $P_0$ played nobody, so $P_0$ is not one of $P_8$'s opponents; hence $P_0$ is $P_8$'s classmate.

Now remove $P_8$ and $P_0$. Every remaining participant played $P_8$ and did not play $P_0$, so each remaining count drops by exactly $1$: $P_1,\dots,P_7$ now have $0,\dots,6$ games, among $8$ people in $4$ classes, where the maximum possible is $6$. The same argument shows that $P_7$ and $P_1$ are classmates. Repeating, $P_6$ and $P_2$ are classmates, and so are $P_5$ and $P_3$.

The remaining two people, $P_4$ and $Q$, form the fifth class, which must be class $A$. At each of the four removals $Q$ played the top person and not the bottom one, so $Q$ played $P_8,P_7,P_6,P_5$ and nobody else: $4$ games. And $P_4$ has $4$ games by definition.

So each participant from class $A$ completed $\boxed{4}$ games.

This situation really occurs: let $P_k$ ($k=5,\dots,8$) play everyone except their classmate $P_{8-k}$ and the people $P_0,\dots,P_{7-k}$; this gives the counts $0,1,\dots,8$, and $Q$ and $P_4$ play exactly $P_5,\dots,P_8$.
