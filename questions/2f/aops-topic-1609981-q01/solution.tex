Clearly $x\ne0$. Dividing by $x^2$,
\[
x^2+x+1+\dfrac{1}{x}+\dfrac{1}{x^2}=0 .
\]
Let $y=x+\dfrac{1}{x}$; then $x^2+\dfrac{1}{x^2}=y^2-2$, and the equation becomes
\[
y^2-2+y+1=0,\qquad y^2+y-1=0,\qquad y=\dfrac{-1\pm\sqrt5}{2}.
\]
Both values occur: for each $y$, the equation $x^2-yx+1=0$ has (complex) roots $x$, and every such $x$ satisfies the quartic. (The roots of the quartic are the fifth roots of unity other than $1$, namely $x=\cos\left(72k\right)^\circ\pm i\sin\left(72k\right)^\circ$ for $k=1,2$, and $x+\dfrac{1}{x}=2\cos72^\circ=\dfrac{\sqrt5-1}{2}$ or $2\cos144^\circ=\dfrac{-1-\sqrt5}{2}$.) So
\[
x+\dfrac{1}{x}=\boxed{\dfrac{-1+\sqrt5}{2}\ \text{or}\ \dfrac{-1-\sqrt5}{2}}.
\]
