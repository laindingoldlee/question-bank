\textbf{Finding $P$.} Let $\alpha=\sqrt2+\sqrt3$. Then $\alpha^2=5+2\sqrt6$, so $\left(\alpha^2-5\right)^2=24$, that is, $\alpha^4-10\alpha^2+1=0$. So
\[P(x)=\boxed{x^4-10x^2+1}\]
works. Its roots are $\pm\sqrt2\pm\sqrt3$, since each of these squares to $5\pm2\sqrt6$.

\textbf{Uniqueness.} If $Q$ were another monic quartic with rational coefficients and $Q(\alpha)=0$, then $R=P-Q$ would be a nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients, of degree at most $3$, with $R(\alpha)=0$. The greatest common divisor of $P$ and $R$ (computed by the Euclidean algorithm, so with rational coefficients) would then have $\alpha$ as a root and degree $1$, $2$ or $3$, giving a factorisation of $P$ into polynomials with rational coefficients of lower degree. A factor of degree $3$ comes with a factor of degree $1$, so it is enough to rule out factors of degree $1$ and $2$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Degree 1.} By the rational root theorem the only possible rational roots are $\pm1$, and $P(\pm1)=-8\ne0$.
\item \emph{Degree 2.} A monic quadratic factor with rational coefficients would have two of the roots $\pm\sqrt2\pm\sqrt3$, with rational sum and product. The sums of two of them are $\pm2\sqrt2$, $\pm2\sqrt3$ or $0$, and the only pairs with sum $0$ are $\pm\left(\sqrt2+\sqrt3\right)$, with product $-\left(5+2\sqrt6\right)$, and $\pm\left(\sqrt2-\sqrt3\right)$, with product $-\left(5-2\sqrt6\right)$. Since $\sqrt2$, $\sqrt3$ and $\sqrt6$ are irrational, none of these works.
\end{itemize}
So $P(x)=x^4-10x^2+1$ is the only such polynomial.
