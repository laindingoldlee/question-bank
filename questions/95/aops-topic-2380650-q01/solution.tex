\textbf{(a)} For positive integers, $\gcd(x,y)\cdot\operatorname{lcm}(x,y)=xy$: for each prime, the exponents in $\gcd$ and $\operatorname{lcm}$ are the minimum and maximum of its exponents in $x$ and $y$, and $\min+\max$ is their sum. So $y=\dfrac{\gcd(x,y)\operatorname{lcm}(x,y)}{x}$ and likewise $z=\dfrac{\gcd(x,z)\operatorname{lcm}(x,z)}{x}$.

\textbf{(b)} The number $x$ divides both $\operatorname{lcm}(x,y)=6$ and $\operatorname{lcm}(x,z)=10$, so $x$ divides $\gcd(6,10)=2$, i.e. $x=1$ or $x=2$. By (a), $y=\dfrac6x$ and $z=\dfrac{10}x$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $x=1$: $(x,y,z)=(1,6,10)$; indeed $\gcd(1,6)=1$, $\operatorname{lcm}(1,6)=6$, $\gcd(1,10)=1$, $\operatorname{lcm}(1,10)=10$;
\item $x=2$: $(x,y,z)=(2,3,5)$; indeed $\gcd(2,3)=1$, $\operatorname{lcm}(2,3)=6$, $\gcd(2,5)=1$, $\operatorname{lcm}(2,5)=10$.
\end{itemize}
So there are exactly two triples, $\boxed{(1,6,10)\text{ and }(2,3,5)}$. Both have the same four values, so in general the gcds and lcms do not determine $x$, $y$, $z$: one also has to know $x$ (which must be a common divisor of the two lcms and a common multiple of the two gcds).
