\textbf{Lemma.} For any positive integer $m$: $\operatorname{lcm}(b,c)\mid m$ if and only if $b\mid m$ and $c\mid m$.

\emph{Proof.} If $L=\operatorname{lcm}(b,c)$ divides $m$, then so do $b$ and $c$, since they divide $L$. Conversely, suppose $b\mid m$ and $c\mid m$, and divide: $m=qL+r$ with $0\le r<L$. Then $r=m-qL$ is divisible by both $b$ and $c$. If $r>0$, it would be a positive common multiple of $b$ and $c$ smaller than $L$, which is impossible. So $r=0$ and $L\mid m$. $\blacksquare$

By the lemma, a positive integer $m$ is a common multiple of $a$ and $\operatorname{lcm}(b,c)$ exactly when $a\mid m$, $b\mid m$ and $c\mid m$, that is, exactly when $m$ is a common multiple of $a$, $b$ and $c$. The two sets of positive common multiples are the same, so their least elements are equal:
\[\operatorname{lcm}(a,b,c)=\operatorname{lcm}\left(a,\operatorname{lcm}(b,c)\right).\ \blacksquare\]

(Alternatively, for each prime $p$, the exponent of $p$ on the left is $\max(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ and on the right $\max\left(\alpha,\max(\beta,\gamma)\right)$, where $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ are the exponents of $p$ in $a,b,c$; these are equal.)
