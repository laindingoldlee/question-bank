\textbf{Difference table.}
\[
\begin{array}{ccccccc}
1 && 5 && 14 && 30\\
& 4 && 9 && 16 &\\
&& 5 && 7 &&\\
&&& 2 &&&
\end{array}
\]
So $f(1)=1$, $\Delta_1=4$, $\Delta_2=5$, $\Delta_3=2$, and all later differences are $0$.

\textbf{The formula.}
\[
f(n)=1+4\dbinom{n-1}{1}+5\dbinom{n-1}{2}+2\dbinom{n-1}{3}.
\]
For $n=10$:
\[
f(10)=1+4\cdot9+5\cdot36+2\cdot84=1+36+180+168=\boxed{385}.
\]

\textbf{Check at $n=5$.} The formula gives $1+16+30+8=55$. Extending the table with constant third difference $2$: the second differences become $5,7,9$, the first differences $4,9,16,25$, and the next term is $30+25=55$. Agreed.

(The sequence is $1^2+2^2+\cdots+n^2$, and indeed $1^2+\cdots+10^2=385$. The formula works because $\dbinom{n-1}{k}$ is exactly the number of ways the $k$-th difference at the start contributes when the table is rebuilt from its left edge, as in Pascal's triangle.)
