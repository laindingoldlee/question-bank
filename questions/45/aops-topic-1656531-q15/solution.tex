\textbf{(a)} For the expression to be defined in the reals we need $4+x\ge0$ and $\sqrt{4+x}\le4$; put $u=\sqrt{4+x}\in[0,4]$. The left side is $g(u)=\sqrt{4-u}+\sqrt{4+u}$, and
\[
g(u)^2=8+2\sqrt{16-u^2}\le8+2\cdot4=16 ,
\]
so $g(u)\le4<6$. Hence there is no real solution.

\textbf{(b)} Squaring once gives $8+2\sqrt{16-(4+x)}=36$, i.e.\ $\sqrt{12-x}=14$, and squaring again gives $x=12-196=-184$. But then $4+x=-180<0$, so $\sqrt{4+x}$ is not a real number: the first squaring step silently used $\sqrt{4-u}\sqrt{4+u}=\sqrt{16-u^2}$ and $u^2=4+x$ for a value of $u$ that does not exist in the reals. So $x=-184$ is an extraneous value, and the equation has $\boxed{\text{no real solution}}$. (With complex numbers and principal square roots, $x=-184$ does satisfy the equation: $\sqrt{4\mp6\sqrt5\,i}=3\mp\sqrt5\,i$, and the two roots add to $6$.)
