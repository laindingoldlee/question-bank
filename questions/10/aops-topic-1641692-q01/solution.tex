\textbf{(a)} The multiples of $p_1$ in $\{1,\dots,n\}$ are
\[
p_1,\ 2p_1,\ 3p_1,\ \dots,\ \dfrac{n}{p_1}\cdot p_1=n ,
\]
that is, $kp_1$ for $k=1,2,\dots,\dfrac{n}{p_1}$ (an integer, since $p_1\mid n$). So there are $\dfrac{n}{p_1}$ of them. The number $\dfrac{n}{p_1}$ is a \emph{count} of multiples; nobody claims that $\dfrac{n}{p_1}$ itself is divisible by $p_1$. (For $n=12$, $p_1=2$: the multiples of $2$ up to $12$ are $2,4,6,8,10,12$, which is $\dfrac{12}{2}=6$ numbers, and $6$ happens to be even, but for $n=10$ the count $\dfrac{10}{2}=5$ is odd.)

\textbf{(b)} An integer $k\le n$ is relatively prime to $n$ exactly when no $p_i$ divides it. Let $A_i$ be the set of $k\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ divisible by $p_i$. For a set $T$ of indices, $k$ lies in every $A_i$ with $i\in T$ exactly when $k$ is divisible by the product $P_T=\prod_{i\in T}p_i$ (distinct primes), and as in (a) there are $\dfrac{n}{P_T}$ such $k$. By inclusion-exclusion,
\[
\varphi(n)=\sum_{T\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}}(-1)^{|T|}\dfrac{n}{P_T}
=n\sum_{T}\prod_{i\in T}\left(-\dfrac{1}{p_i}\right)
=n\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-\dfrac{1}{p_i}\right),
\]
where the last step is the expansion of the product (each term of the expansion picks $1$ or $-\dfrac{1}{p_i}$ from each factor, and the indices where $-\dfrac{1}{p_i}$ is picked form $T$). $\blacksquare$

For example, $\varphi(12)=12\left(1-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\left(1-\dfrac{1}{3}\right)=4$: the numbers $1,5,7,11$.
