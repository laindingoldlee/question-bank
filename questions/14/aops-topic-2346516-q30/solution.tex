Work backwards from the end.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Emily alone} keeps all $100$ coins.
\item \textbf{Dan and Emily.} Dan votes for his own proposal, which is $1$ of $2$ votes, a tie, so it passes. Dan proposes Dan $100$, Emily $0$.
\item \textbf{Charlie, Dan, Emily.} Charlie needs $2$ of $3$ votes. Emily gets $0$ if Charlie dies, so $1$ coin buys her vote: Charlie $99$, Dan $0$, Emily $1$.
\item \textbf{Beth, Charlie, Dan, Emily.} Beth needs $2$ of $4$ votes, so one vote besides her own. Dan gets $0$ if Beth dies, so $1$ coin buys his vote: Beth $99$, Charlie $0$, Dan $1$, Emily $0$.
\item \textbf{All five.} Amar needs $3$ of $5$ votes, so two votes besides his own. The cheapest are Charlie and Emily, who get $0$ if Amar dies, so $1$ coin each buys their votes (anyone else would need at least $2$ coins, and Beth would need $100$).
\end{itemize}
So Amar should propose
\[\boxed{\text{Amar }98,\ \text{Beth }0,\ \text{Charlie }1,\ \text{Dan }0,\ \text{Emily }1},\]
which passes with the votes of Amar, Charlie and Emily, and he keeps $98$ coins.
