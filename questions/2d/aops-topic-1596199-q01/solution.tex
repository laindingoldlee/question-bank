There are finitely many ways to write $n$ as such a sum, so the maximum exists. We show which sums attain it.

\textbf{Small cases.} $P(1)=1$ (the only sum is $1$), $P(2)=2$, $P(3)=3$, $P(4)=4$ ($4$ or $2+2$).

\textbf{Shape of a best sum for $n\ge2$.} Take a sum with the largest product. We may change it in the following ways without decreasing the product, so we may assume none of them applies.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{No part $1$.} If there is a part $1$ and another part $m$ (there is one, as $n\ge2$), replace $1$ and $m$ by the single part $m+1$: the product is multiplied by $\dfrac{m+1}{m}>1$. So a best sum has no $1$s.
\item \emph{No part $k\ge5$.} Replace $k$ by $2$ and $k-2$: since $2(k-2)=2k-4>k$ for $k>4$, the product increases. So a best sum has no part larger than $4$.
\item \emph{No part $4$.} Replace $4$ by $2+2$; the product is unchanged, so we may assume there are no $4$s.
\item \emph{At most two $2$s.} Replace $2+2+2$ by $3+3$: $3\cdot3=9>8=2\cdot2\cdot2$.
\end{itemize}
So a best sum consists of $3$s and at most two $2$s, and the number of $2$s is fixed by $n$ modulo $3$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $n=3k$: no $2$s, product $3^{k}$;
\item $n=3k+2$: one $2$, product $2\cdot3^{k}$;
\item $n=3k+1$ with $k\ge1$: two $2$s, product $4\cdot3^{k-1}$.
\end{itemize}
Therefore
\[
\boxed{P(n)=\begin{cases}3^{k}, & n=3k,\\ 4\cdot3^{k-1}, & n=3k+1\ \ (k\ge1),\\ 2\cdot3^{k}, & n=3k+2,\end{cases}\qquad P(1)=1.}
\]
For example $P(10)=4\cdot3^2=36$ (from $3+3+2+2$) and $P(12)=3^4=81$.

\textbf{Remarks.} The restriction to \emph{positive integers} matters. If negative parts were allowed the product would be unbounded (for example $n=(-m)+(-m)+(2m+n)$ gives the product $m^2(2m+n)$). If any positive real parts were allowed, the best product with $k$ equal parts $\dfrac {n}{k}$ is $\left(\dfrac {n}{k}\right)^k$, and the maximum over $k$ is attained when $k$ is close to $\dfrac {n}{e}$.
