Choose the one person who gets the correct meal: $9$ ways. By symmetry we may assume this person ordered beef. Now the remaining people are $2$ beef-orderers, $3$ chicken-orderers and $3$ fish-orderers, the remaining meals are $2$ beef, $3$ chicken and $3$ fish, and nobody may get the meal they ordered. Consider which meals the two beef-orderers get.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Both get chicken.} The remaining meals are $2$ beef, $1$ chicken, $3$ fish. The fish-orderers cannot get fish, so they get exactly the $2$ beef and $1$ chicken ($3$ ways to decide who gets the chicken), and the chicken-orderers get the $3$ fish. $3$ ways.
\item \textbf{Both get fish.} Symmetrically, $3$ ways.
\item \textbf{One gets chicken, one gets fish} ($2$ ways). The remaining meals are $2$ beef, $2$ chicken, $2$ fish. The $2$ fish meals must go to chicken-orderers and the $2$ chicken meals to fish-orderers, so one chicken-orderer and one fish-orderer get the beef meals: $3\cdot3=9$ choices. $2\cdot9=18$ ways.
\end{itemize}
That is $3+3+18=24$ ways for each choice of the lucky person, so the answer is $9\cdot24=\boxed{216}$.

The posted argument is not valid: ``$3!$ ways to serve one type of dish'' does not describe any count in this problem (the meals of one type are identical, and the condition ``exactly one person is served correctly'' couples the three types), so multiplying three such factors has no justification. It is a coincidence that $6^3=216=9\cdot24$.
