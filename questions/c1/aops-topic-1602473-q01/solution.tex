\textbf{The remainder.} Divide: $x^{51}+51=(x+1)q(x)+r$, where the remainder $r$ is a constant because the divisor has degree $1$. Putting $x=-1$, which makes the divisor zero, gives
\[
(-1)^{51}+51=0\cdot q(-1)+r,\qquad\text{so}\qquad r=-1+51=\boxed{50}.
\]
This is the remainder theorem: the remainder on division of $p(x)$ by $x-a$ is $p(a)$; here $a=-1$.

\textbf{The error.} Polynomial division is an identity of polynomials, $x^{51}+51=(x+1)q(x)+50$, where $q$ has integer coefficients. Substituting $x=1$ gives the true statement $52=2q(1)+50$, i.e.\ $q(1)=1$; it does not say that the remainder of the \emph{integer} $52$ on division by $2$ is $50$. Integer remainders and polynomial remainders are different things: substituting a number for $x$ turns the polynomial quotient $q(1)$ and remainder $50$ into an integer quotient and ``remainder'' that need not satisfy $0\le r<2$. So the integer division $52\div2$ tells us nothing about the polynomial remainder.
