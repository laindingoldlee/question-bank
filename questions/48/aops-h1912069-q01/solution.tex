Put $s=a+b+c=a^2+b^2+c^2$. Cauchy-Schwarz gives $s^2\le3s$, so $s\in\{0,1,2,3\}$. If $s=0$, all variables are zero and $(m,n)=(0,1)$ works. If $s=3$, equality in Cauchy-Schwarz forces $a=b=c=1$, and $(m,n)=(1,1)$ works.

Suppose $s=1$. Then $ab+bc+ca=0$. If the product is zero, use $(m,n)=(0,1)$. Otherwise $b\ne0$ and $a+b\ne0$. Write $t=\dfrac{a}{b}=\dfrac{p}{q}$ with coprime integers $p,q$, $q\ne0$. Solving $c=-\dfrac{ab}{a+b}$ together with $a+b+c=1$ gives, for $D=p^2+pq+q^2>0$,
\[
a=\dfrac{p(p+q)}{D},\quad b=\dfrac{q(p+q)}{D},\quad c=-\dfrac{pq}{D}.
\]
Consequently
\[
abc=-\dfrac{[pq(p+q)]^2}{D^3}.
\]
Every prime dividing $D$ divides none of $p,q,p+q$: otherwise the equation for $D$ would force it to divide both $p$ and $q$. Thus $m=|pq(p+q)|$ and $n=-D$ are coprime and have the required form.

If $s=2$, the complementary triple $(1-a,1-b,1-c)$ has both sum and square-sum $1$. Its pairwise-product sum is zero, so
\[
abc=1-\sum(1-a)+\sum(1-a)(1-b)-(1-a)(1-b)(1-c)
=-(1-a)(1-b)(1-c).
\]
Apply the preceding case and reverse the sign of $n$. This completes all possibilities.
