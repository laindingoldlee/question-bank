\textbf{No.} Consider the triangles with sides $17,25,28$ and $20,21,29$.
\begin{itemize}
\item Both have perimeter $70$.
\item Triangle $ABC$ with $A=(0,0)$, $B=(28,0)$, $C=(8,15)$ has $AC=\sqrt{64+225}=17$ and $BC=\sqrt{400+225}=25$, so its sides are $17,25,28$; its height on side $AB$ is $15$, so its area is $\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot28\cdot15=210$.
\item Since $20^2+21^2=841=29^2$, the second triangle is right-angled with legs $20$ and $21$, so its area is $\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot20\cdot21=210$.
\end{itemize}
The side lengths differ, so the triangles are not congruent.

\textbf{Why such pairs are common.} Fix a side $BC=a$ and the perimeter. Then $AB+AC$ is fixed, so $A$ lies on an ellipse with foci $B$ and $C$. Fixing the area fixes the height from $A$, so $A$ also lies on a line parallel to $BC$. Such a line usually meets the ellipse in points giving one triangle up to reflection; but we may change the side $a$ as well. In general one equation for the perimeter and one for the area (by Heron's formula, $(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)$ fixed with $s$ fixed) leave a one-parameter family of triangles, which contains non-congruent members. Only special triangles (such as the equilateral triangle, which has the largest area for its perimeter) are determined by perimeter and area.
