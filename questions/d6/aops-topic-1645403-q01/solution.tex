\textbf{(a)} Complete the square and use the difference of squares:
\[
x^4+y^4=(x^2+y^2)^2-2x^2y^2=(x^2+y^2)^2-(\sqrt2\,xy)^2=\boxed{\left(x^2+\sqrt2\,xy+y^2\right)\left(x^2-\sqrt2\,xy+y^2\right)}.
\]
Check: the product is $(x^2+y^2)^2-2x^2y^2=x^4+2x^2y^2+y^4-2x^2y^2$.

\textbf{(b)} Suppose $x^4+y^4=F(x,y)G(x,y)$ with non-constant integer polynomials. Then $F$ and $G$ are homogeneous (otherwise the product of the highest-degree parts and of the lowest-degree parts would be two different homogeneous parts of $x^4+y^4$), and each contains a pure power of $x$, because the product contains $x^4$. Putting $y=1$ gives a factorisation of $x^4+1$ into integer polynomials of the same positive degrees.

A linear factor would give a real root of $x^4+1$, which has none (it is always at least $1$). So it would be $x^4+1=(x^2+ax+b)(x^2+cx+d)$ with integers $a,b,c,d$. Comparing coefficients: $a+c=0$ ($x^3$), $ad+bc=0$ ($x$), $bd=1$ and $b+d+ac=0$ ($x^2$). From $bd=1$, $b=d=\pm1$, and with $c=-a$ the $x^2$ condition becomes $2b=a^2$, so $a^2=\pm2$, which has no integer solution. Hence no such factorisation exists.
