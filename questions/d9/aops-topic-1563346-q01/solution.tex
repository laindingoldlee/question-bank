\textbf{At least $10$.} Each piece covers $4$ squares, so at least $\dfrac{36}{4}=9$ pieces are needed. With exactly $9$ pieces the pieces cover exactly the $36$ squares of the grid, i.e.\ they tile the $6\times6$ square with no overhang. Colour the grid like a chessboard: $18$ black and $18$ white squares. Every T-piece covers $3$ squares of one colour and $1$ of the other (its three-in-a-row part alternates colours, and the middle square's neighbour has the colour of the two ends). If $k$ of the $9$ pieces cover three black squares, the number of black squares covered is $3k+(9-k)=2k+9$, which is odd and cannot equal $18$. So $9$ pieces are impossible.

\textbf{$10$ pieces suffice.} The figure shows a covering by $10$ pieces; the grid is outlined in red, and pieces $1$ and $10$ stick out at two corners.
