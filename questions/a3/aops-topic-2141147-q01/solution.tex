Apply $j$ square roots to $4\cdot4=16$:
\[\underbrace{\sqrt{\sqrt{\cdots\sqrt{4\cdot4}}}}_{j\text{ roots}}=16^{\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{j}}=4^{2\cdot2^{-j}}=4^{2^{1-j}} .\]
Taking $\log_4$ of this gives $2^{1-j}$, and taking $\log_4$ again gives
\[\log_4 2^{1-j}=(1-j)\log_42=\dfrac{1-j}{2}.\]
Therefore
\[-\log_4\left(\log_4\underbrace{\sqrt{\sqrt{\cdots\sqrt{4\cdot4}}}}_{j\text{ roots}}\right)=\dfrac{j-1}{2},\]
an expression that uses exactly four $4$s (two inside the roots and two as bases of logarithms). Choosing $j=2n+1$ gives the value $n$, for every positive integer $n$. In particular, $3764376$ is obtained with $j=2\cdot3764376+1=7528753$ square roots. So the answer is \textbf{yes}.

\emph{Remark.} This is a version of a trick attributed to Paul Dirac. Without logarithms the trick is not available, and whether a particular number can be reached becomes a matter of searching through expressions.
