\textbf{(a)} Let the points be $P_1=(x_1,y_1)\ne P_2=(x_2,y_2)$. A line is $ax+by=c$ with $(a,b)\ne(0,0)$. It contains both points exactly when $(a,b)\cdot(x_2-x_1,y_2-y_1)=0$ and $c=ax_1+by_1$. The first condition says that $(a,b)$ is a nonzero multiple of $\left(-(y_2-y_1),\,x_2-x_1\right)$, and then $c$ is determined. Multiplying $a,b,c$ by the same nonzero number gives the same line, so there is exactly one line through $P_1$ and $P_2$. $\blacksquare$

\textbf{(b)} The centre $O$ of a circle through $A,B,C$ satisfies $OA=OB=OC$, so it lies on the perpendicular bisectors of $AB$ and $BC$. If $A,B,C$ are not collinear, these bisectors are perpendicular to the non-parallel lines $AB$ and $BC$, so they meet in exactly one point $O$. The circle with centre $O$ and radius $OA$ passes through all three points, and it is the only one. If $A,B,C$ are distinct and collinear, both bisectors are perpendicular to the same line and pass through the different midpoints of $AB$ and $BC$, so they are parallel and distinct, and no centre exists. $\blacksquare$

\end{asy}

\textbf{(c)} Both parabolas contain $(0,-1)$ and $(-1,0)$: $(-1)=0^2-1$, $0=(-1)^2-1$, and symmetrically. For a point $(t,t)$ both equations read $t=t^2-1$, whose roots are $\varphi$ and $1-\varphi$. So the four points lie on two different parabolas. $\blacksquare$

\textbf{(d)} An ellipse bounds a strictly convex region, so none of its points is inside the convex hull of other points of the ellipse. But $(1,1)$ is strictly inside the quadrilateral with vertices $(-1,-1)$, $\left(2,\dfrac12\right)$, $\left(\dfrac12,2\right)$, $\left(-2,-\dfrac12\right)$ (taken in counterclockwise order, $(1,1)$ is strictly to the left of each of the four directed sides). Hence no ellipse contains all five points. (They lie on the hyperbola $xy=1$, and in fact on no other conic.) $\blacksquare$

So only the first two claims are correct (the second for non-collinear points).
