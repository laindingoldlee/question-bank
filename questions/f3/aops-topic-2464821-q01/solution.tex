There are $2\cdot6=12$ equally likely outcomes (coin, die), such as (H,$3$).

\textbf{(a)} Only (H,$6$) works, so the probability is $\dfrac1{12}$. This equals $\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac16$: half of the $12$ outcomes have heads, and among those, one sixth have a $6$. Multiplying is correct here because the coin and the die are \emph{independent}: knowing the coin result does not change the chances for the die. So $P(\text{H and }6)=\boxed{\dfrac1{12}}$.

\textbf{(b)} The events ``$5$'' and ``$6$'' cannot happen together (they are \emph{mutually exclusive}), so the outcomes counted for them do not overlap and the probabilities add:
\[P(5\text{ or }6)=\dfrac16+\dfrac16=\boxed{\dfrac13}.\]

\textbf{(c)} Heads and $6$ can happen together, so adding $\dfrac12+\dfrac16$ would count the outcome (H,$6$) twice. Subtracting it once (inclusion--exclusion):
\[P(\text{H or }6)=\dfrac12+\dfrac16-\dfrac1{12}=\boxed{\dfrac7{12}}.\]
Check by counting: the $6$ outcomes with heads and the outcome (T,$6$) make $7$ of the $12$.

\textbf{Summary.} ``And'' for independent events: multiply. ``Or'' for events that cannot happen together: add. ``Or'' for events that can happen together: add and subtract the probability of both.
