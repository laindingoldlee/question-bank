The diagonals of a kite are perpendicular. They split the kite into four right triangles, and adding up their areas shows that the area of a kite is half the product of its diagonals:
\[
\text{Area}=\dfrac{d_1d_2}{2}.
\]

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(6cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
path ra(pair P,pair u,pair v,real s=0.2){return P+s*unit(u)--P+s*unit(u)+s*unit(v)--P+s*unit(v);}
pair A=(0,0),B=(4,2.5),C=(16,0),D=(4,-2.5),O=(4,0);
draw(A--B--C--D--cycle,linewidth(0.9));
draw(A--C,blue); draw(B--D,red);
draw(ra(O,C-O,B-O,0.7));
draw(B--(-1.3,2.5),gray+dotted); draw(D--(-1.3,-2.5),gray+dotted);
draw(A--(0,-3.8),gray+dotted); draw(C--(16,-3.8),gray+dotted);
draw((-1,-2.5)--(-1,2.5),red,Arrows(TeXHead)); draw((0,-3.5)--(16,-3.5),blue,Arrows(TeXHead));
label("$5$",(-1,0),W,red); label("$16$",(8,-3.5),S,blue);
label("\mbox{}",(min(currentpicture,user=true)+max(currentpicture,user=true))/2,black); // keeps the text after the figure black when LaTeX typesets the labels inline
\end{asy}

So $\dfrac{5\cdot d_2}{2}=40$, which gives $d_2=\boxed{16}$ inches. (The figure shows one such kite, with the perpendicular diagonals $5$ and $16$.)
