Use $T(x)=\dfrac{x(x+1)(2x+1)}{6}$, or simply add the next square each time, and compare with nearby squares:
\[\begin{array}{c|c|c||c|c|c}
x & T(x) & \text{between} & x & T(x) & \text{between}\\\hline
2 & 5 & 2^2,\ 3^2 & 14 & 1015 & 31^2,\ 32^2\\
3 & 14 & 3^2,\ 4^2 & 15 & 1240 & 35^2,\ 36^2\\
4 & 30 & 5^2,\ 6^2 & 16 & 1496 & 38^2,\ 39^2\\
5 & 55 & 7^2,\ 8^2 & 17 & 1785 & 42^2,\ 43^2\\
6 & 91 & 9^2,\ 10^2 & 18 & 2109 & 45^2,\ 46^2\\
7 & 140 & 11^2,\ 12^2 & 19 & 2470 & 49^2,\ 50^2\\
8 & 204 & 14^2,\ 15^2 & 20 & 2870 & 53^2,\ 54^2\\
9 & 285 & 16^2,\ 17^2 & 21 & 3311 & 57^2,\ 58^2\\
10 & 385 & 19^2,\ 20^2 & 22 & 3795 & 61^2,\ 62^2\\
11 & 506 & 22^2,\ 23^2 & 23 & 4324 & 65^2,\ 66^2\\
12 & 650 & 25^2,\ 26^2 & 24 & 4900 & =70^2\\
13 & 819 & 28^2,\ 29^2 & & &
\end{array}\]
None of $T(2),\ldots,T(23)$ is a square, while
\[T(24)=\dfrac{24\cdot25\cdot49}{6}=4900=70^2.\]
So the smallest such $x>1$ is $\boxed{24}$. In particular the answer to ``is $T(x)$ ever a perfect square for $x>1$?'' is yes.

(It is a famous theorem, proved by G.~N.~Watson in 1918, that $x=1$ and $x=24$ are the \emph{only} solutions; the proof is far harder. This is the ``cannonball problem'': $4900$ cannonballs can be stacked as a square pyramid $24$ layers high and also laid out as a $70\times70$ square.)
