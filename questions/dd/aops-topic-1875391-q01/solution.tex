\textbf{Why substitution works (remainder theorem).} If a polynomial $p(x)$ is divided by $x-a$, then $p(x)=(x-a)q(x)+r$ for a polynomial $q(x)$ and a constant $r$ (the remainder has lower degree than $x-a$). Putting $x=a$ kills the first term, so $r=p(a)$.

Here $p(x)=f(x+1)=(x+1)^3+k$ and $x+1=x-(-1)$, so $a=-1$ and the remainder is
\[
p(-1)=f(-1+1)=f(0)=k .
\]
(Directly: $(x+1)^3+k=(x+1)\cdot(x+1)^2+k$.) Since the remainder is $1$, $k=\boxed{1}$.

The substitution $u=x+1$ in the poster's own argument is also correct: dividing $f(x+1)$ by $x+1$ is dividing $f(u)$ by $u$, whose remainder is $f(0)$.
