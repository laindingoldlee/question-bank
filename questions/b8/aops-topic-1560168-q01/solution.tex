\textbf{(a)} The $n$th digit of the random key is one of $10$ equally likely digits, and exactly one of them is your $n$th digit. So the probability is $\boxed{\dfrac{1}{10}}$, whatever happens in the other positions.

\textbf{(b)} Choose the matching position ($6$ ways); it matches with probability $\dfrac{1}{10}$ and each of the other five positions fails to match with probability $\dfrac{9}{10}$, independently. So
\[
P(\text{exactly one match})=6\cdot\dfrac{1}{10}\cdot\left(\dfrac{9}{10}\right)^5=\dfrac{6\cdot9^5}{10^6}=\boxed{\dfrac{177147}{500000}}\approx0.354 .
\]

\textbf{(c)} The number $\dfrac{1}{60}$ has no basis: it would be $\dfrac{1}{6}\cdot\dfrac{1}{10}$, but there is no extra factor $\dfrac{1}{6}$ for ``choosing'' the position, because the position $n$ is fixed in advance. The number $\dfrac{6\cdot9^5}{10^6}$ is the answer to a different question, part (b): the probability that \emph{exactly one} (unspecified) position matches. The probability that a \emph{given} position matches is simply $\dfrac{1}{10}$.
