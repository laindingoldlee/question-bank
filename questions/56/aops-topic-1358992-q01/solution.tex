No such triangle exists. By the triangle inequality, every side of a triangle is shorter than the sum of the other two, so we would need $AB<AC+BC=15$. But
\[
AB=\sqrt{263}>\sqrt{225}=15 .
\]
Geometrically: $A$ would have to lie on the circle with centre $C$ and radius $5$ and on the circle with centre $B$ and radius $\sqrt{263}$. Every point of the first circle is at distance at most $10+5=15<\sqrt{263}$ from $B$, so the circles do not meet.

\begin{asy}
usepackage("amsmath");
size(6.5cm);
defaultpen(fontsize(10pt));
pair B=(0,0),C=(10,0);
real R=sqrt(263);
draw(arc(B,R,-38,38),blue+dashed);
draw(circle(C,5),red+dashed);
draw(B--C,linewidth(0.9));
dot(B);dot(C);
label("$B$",B,W);label("$C$",C,S);
label("$10$",(3,0),S);
draw(C--C+5*dir(60),red); label("$5$",C+2.5*dir(60),W,red);
label("radius $\sqrt{263}$",B+R*dir(38),W,blue);
label("\mbox{}",(min(currentpicture,user=true)+max(currentpicture,user=true))/2,black); // keeps the text after the figure black when LaTeX typesets the labels inline
\end{asy}

(Heron's formula would also signal the problem: with $s=\dfrac{15+\sqrt{263}}{2}$, the factor $s-AB=\dfrac{15-\sqrt{263}}{2}$ is negative, so $16[ABC]^2$ would be negative.)
