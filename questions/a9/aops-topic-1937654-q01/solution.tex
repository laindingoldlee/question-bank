(a) By the binomial theorem, $(n+1)^p-n^p=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p}\dbinom pi n^{p-i}$. Summing over $n=1,\dots,k$, the left side telescopes to $(k+1)^p-1$:
\[
(k+1)^p-1=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\dbinom pi S_{p-i}(k)=p\,S_{p-1}(k)+\sum_{i=2}^{p}\dbinom pi S_{p-i}(k),
\]
which rearranges to the identity.

(b) Take $p=2$, $k=1$: the student's version says $S_1(1)=2^2-1-S_0(1)=2$, but $S_1(1)=1$. (The correct version gives $2S_1(1)=2$.)

(c) Use $S_0=k$, $S_1=\dfrac{k(k+1)}{2}$, $S_2=\dfrac{k(k+1)(2k+1)}{6}$. With $p=4$,
\[
4S_3=(k+1)^4-1-6S_2-4S_1-S_0=(k+1)^4-(k+1)-k(k+1)(2k+1)-2k(k+1).
\]
Factor out $k+1$: the bracket is $(k+1)^3-1-k(2k+1)-2k=k^3+3k^2+3k-2k^2-k-2k=k^3+k^2=k^2(k+1)$. So $4S_3=k^2(k+1)^2$ and
\[
S_3(k)=\boxed{\left(\dfrac{k(k+1)}{2}\right)^2}.
\]
(Repeating this process gives every $S_p$ as a polynomial in $k$ of degree $p+1$; the general closed form is Faulhaber's formula, which involves the Bernoulli numbers.)
