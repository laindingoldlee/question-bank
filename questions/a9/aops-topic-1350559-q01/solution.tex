Consider $p=a^2-b^3$ and suppose $p=gh$ with $g,h$ non-constant real polynomials. View all polynomials as polynomials in $a$ whose coefficients are polynomials in $b$. The degrees in $a$ add up: $\deg_a g+\deg_a h=2$.

If, say, $\deg_a g=0$, then $g=g(b)$ is a polynomial in $b$ alone that divides every coefficient of $p$ as a polynomial in $a$; in particular it divides the coefficient $1$ of $a^2$, so $g$ is constant, a contradiction. Hence $\deg_a g=\deg_a h=1$: $g=g_1(b)a+g_0(b)$ and $h=h_1(b)a+h_0(b)$. Comparing the coefficients of $a^2$ gives $g_1h_1=1$, so $g_1$ and $h_1$ are nonzero constants, and after rescaling we may take $g=a+u(b)$, $h=a+v(b)$. Then
\[
(a+u)(a+v)=a^2+(u+v)a+uv=a^2-b^3
\]
forces $v=-u$ and $u(b)^2=b^3$. This is impossible: the degree of $u^2$ is even, while $b^3$ has degree $3$. So $a^2-b^3$ cannot be factored.

The same argument works for the others: for $a^2+b^3$ it would need $u(b)^2=-b^3$, again of odd degree; and $a^3-b^2$ and $a^3+b^2$ are, as polynomials in $b$, of the form $-(b^2-a^3)$ and $b^2+a^3$, which need $u(a)^2=a^3$ or $u(a)^2=-a^3$. Hence $\boxed{\text{none of the four can be factored}}$. (Compare $a^3-b^3=(a-b)\left(a^2+ab+b^2\right)$ and $a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)$, where the exponents match.)
